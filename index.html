Chapter 1 – Introduction and Context

1.1 Why Agentic AI Matters for BFSI Now

Over the last decade, AI in BFSI has moved from isolated models embedded in specific workflows to distributed intelligence that can autonomously orchestrate entire financial processes.
The emergence of agentic AI—networks of AI agents capable of taking actions, calling APIs, coordinating with other systems and escalating to humans—marks a structural shift from “AI that suggests” to “AI that executes” in regulated financial environments.

In practice, leading institutions are already deploying multiagent systems for complex workflows such as trade finance documentation analysis, credit lifecycle management, onboarding, AML, collections and crossborder transaction processing.
Recent landscape analyses suggest that BFSI may account for around onethird of observed agentic AI use cases globally, reflecting the sector’s combination of high transaction volumes, rulerich processes and strong regulatory oversight.

For India’s BFSI sector, this evolution coincides with a regulatory inflection point: NITI Aayog’s AI for All ethos has crystallised into Responsible AI guidance, and the Reserve Bank of India has released its Framework for Responsible and Ethical Enablement of AI (FREEAI), a 26point roadmap anchored in seven core Sutras and six strategic pillars.
The question is no longer whether AI will be used in BFSI, but how agentic AI can be scaled responsibly—with trust, fairness, explainability, safety and accountability as nonnegotiable design constraints.

1.2 Defining Agentic AI in Financial Services

For the purposes of this report, we use “agentic AI” to describe AI systems that:

  • Operate as autonomous or semiautonomous agents capable of taking actions (not just generating outputs) within financial workflows—submitting forms, triggering transactions, updating records, initiating communications.
  • Interact with external tools and systems through APIs, RPA connectors and enterprise integrations, rather than remaining confined to a single application.
  • Maintain longerterm context and memory, enabling multistep workflows (e.g., endtoend loan origination or claims handling) rather than singlestep predictions.
  • Collaborate with other agents in multiagent ecosystems, for example separate agents responsible for document extraction, risk scoring, sanctions screening and customer messaging within one composite process.

Traditional AI in BFSI largely focused on predictive tasks—credit scoring, fraud detection, churn models—embedded within humanled processes.
Agentic AI systems, by contrast, own the workflow, handling orchestration and decisions up to a defined risk boundary, with humans intheloop or ontheloop for exceptions and highimpact cases.

This shift magnifies both upside and downside: agentic AI promises orders of magnitude improvements in speed, consistency and cost, but also introduces new systemic risks around misaligned autonomy, opaque decision chains, emergent behaviour and concentrated technological dependencies.

1.3 HighImpact Agentic AI Use Cases Across BFSI

While this report will examine use cases in detail in later chapters, it is useful to highlight the clusters of highimpact agentic AI applications that are already being piloted or deployed in BFSI.

Retail and Corporate Banking

  • Endtoend digital lending agents that collect applications conversationally, extract data from income documents and bank statements, pull bureau reports, run policy checks and generate credit memos—often enabling sameday decisions for standard products.
  • Collection and recovery agents that orchestrate reminders, negotiate repayment plans, assess intent to pay and escalate highrisk accounts, operating continuously across channels.
  • Customer service copilot agents that assist retail customers with KYC, document upload, eligibility checks, account changes and billing queries, reducing load on contact centres while maintaining journey continuity.

Capital Markets and Wealth

  • Autonomous trading and portfolio agents that manage rebalancing, liquidity and risk constraints in real time, coordinating signals from market data, news and macro indicators within defined guardrails.
  • Research and advisory agents (e.g., multiagent “Ask David”style systems) that automate data gathering, screening and preliminary analysis for relationship managers and private bankers.

Insurance

  • Zerotouch claims agents that classify claims, analyse photos or videos, extract and validate documentation, apply adjudication rules and trigger payments for lowvalue, lowrisk cases.
  • Policy recommendation and onboarding agents that compare products, personalise coverage suggestions, handle onboarding and renewals, and manage addon recommendations for both individuals and groups.

Risk, Compliance and Operations

  • KYC/AML orchestration agents that coordinate identity verification, sanctions and PEP screening, risk scoring, enhanced due diligence triggers and ongoing monitoring, while maintaining regulatorready audit logs.
  • Regulatory intelligence agents that track regulatory changes, map them to internal policies and generate draft impact assessments and policy updates for human review.
  • Document and reporting agents that automate MIS, liquidity forecasts, internal board reports and regulatory filings by extracting and validating data across fragmented systems.

Across these use cases, a pattern emerges: agentic AI is being applied first to highvolume, rulerich, multistep workflows where human effort is largely procedural rather than judgmentheavy.
This is precisely the terrain where robust Responsible AI and FREEAI alignment are essential, because missteps can propagate quickly and invisibly through the financial system.

1.4 The Need for a Responsible, PrincipleLed Approach

India’s AI policy trajectory has consistently emphasised that widespread AI adoption must be grounded in ethics, trust and inclusion, not just technical performance.
NITI Aayog’s National Strategy on AI and subsequent “Responsible AI for All” papers frame AI for All as the governing benchmark for AI design, development and deployment—emphasising fairness, transparency, safety, accountability, privacy and humancentricity.

Responsible AI frameworks developed globally, and adapted in the Indian context, stress common themes:

  • Fairness and nondiscrimination in outcomes, particularly in credit, pricing, underwriting and claims.
  • Transparency and explainability, so that institutions understand and can communicate how AI systems reach decisions—especially in highstakes settings.
  • Safety, resilience and security, recognising new attack surfaces such as prompt injection, data poisoning and model theft in AI systems.
  • Accountability, ensuring deploying institutions remain responsible for AI outcomes regardless of autonomy or vendor involvement.

As agentic AI moves from pilots to production, these principles shift from abstract policy to engineering and operational constraints.
An agent that can autonomously approve loans, settle claims or update portfolios must embed fairness checks, override mechanisms, logging, auditability and robust data governance; otherwise, the very autonomy that creates value becomes a source of unacceptable risk.

In this context, RBI’s FREEAI framework does not simply add another compliance layer; it provides a sectorspecific backbone to operationalise Responsible AI in BFSI, calibrated to India’s regulatory, infrastructural and inclusion priorities.

1.5 RBI’s FREEAI Framework as the Sector Backbone

The FREEAI framework is the most comprehensive AI governance blueprint issued so far by an Indian financial regulator, expressly aimed at guiding AI adoption in banks, NBFCs, payment system operators and other regulated entities.
Developed through extensive consultations and surveys across hundreds of stakeholders, it distils AI ethics and prudential concerns into seven Sutras and six strategic pillars that underpin 26 recommendations.

The seven Sutras—Trust is the Foundation, People First, Innovation over Restraint, Fairness and Equity, Accountability, Understandable by Design, Safety/Resilience/Sustainability—set the normative expectations for any AI system in BFSI, regardless of technology stack or use case.
They are particularly salient for agentic AI, where the temptation is to let autonomous systems gradually displace human judgment under the guise of efficiency; FREEAI insists that trust, human primacy, fairness and explainability remain nonnegotiable.

Operationally, FREEAI is organised into two subframeworks—Innovation Enablement and Risk Mitigation—anchored in six pillars:

  • Innovation Enablement: Infrastructure, Policy, Capacity (shared data and AI infrastructure; agile policies; sectorwide skills and knowledge).
  • Risk Mitigation: Governance, Protection, Assurance (clear accountability, consumer safeguards, independent validation and continuous monitoring).

The 26 recommendations span tasks such as creating sectoral data infrastructure and AI sandboxes, building institutional AI policies and inventories, strengthening fairness and explainability practices, and evolving AIspecific audit and assurance mechanisms.

For agentic AI, FREEAI’s message can be summarised as follows:

  • Autonomy does not dilute accountability—entities remain responsible for AI decisions even when systems act without manual triggers.
  • Innovation is encouraged, but only when paired with robust governance, protection and assurance—responsible innovation, not restraint, is the goal.
  • Infrastructure, policy and capacity must be upgraded in parallel with technology, otherwise agentic AI deployments will outpace institutions’ ability to understand, monitor and correct them.

This report therefore treats FREEAI as the central reference point: agentic AI use cases are analysed through the lens of the Sutras and pillars, and our operational guidance is framed as practical pathways for BFSI institutions to become “FREEAI aligned” while drawing on NITI RA and global frameworks.

1.6 Purpose and Audience of This Report

The purpose of this report is to provide a practitioneroriented operating manual for agentic AI in India’s BFSI sector—one that goes beyond abstract principles to concrete patterns, controls and examples.
We focus on highimpact agentic AI use cases that are already emerging or are likely to be adopted in the medium term, and show what it takes to deploy them responsibly under FREEAI, NITI RA and related regulatory expectations.

The primary audiences are:

  • BFSI technologists and architects, who are designing and integrating agentic AI systems into complex legacy and modern stacks.
  • Chief AI Officers and Heads of Data/Risk, who must reconcile innovation ambitions with prudential, conduct and consumerprotection responsibilities.
  • Vendor and platform ecosystems, including hyperscalers, AI tool providers and system integrators, whose products will shape the technical envelope of BFSI agentic AI.
  • Fintech startups, particularly those building agentic workflows for credit, payments, investments and insurance, who need to design for regulatory alignment from day zero.

The analysis is informed by primary research (qualitative interviews and quantitative surveys with BFSI leaders and practitioners) and secondary research (FREEAI, NITI RA, DPDP, sectoral regulations, and international RAI frameworks).
Throughout, the tone is deliberately technical and systemsoriented: we assume readers understand both AI architectures and regulatory vocabulary, and are looking for actionable guidance rather than highlevel advocacy.

Chapter 2 – Principles and Regulatory Compass for Agentic AI in BFSI

2.1 Why a Compass Is Essential When Signals Are Mixed

In our interviews and survey responses, AI leaders in BFSI consistently express a dual sentiment: they see agentic AI as a structural lever for scale and efficiency, yet they are equally worried about opacity, bias, regulatory exposure and loss of control.
This tension is not surprising—agentic AI moves us from modeldriven decision support to autonomous, multiagent systems that can initiate actions, orchestrate workflows and interact with external tools, often across organisational and jurisdictional boundaries.

In such an environment, neither pure enthusiasm nor pure caution is sufficient. BFSI technologists, Chief AI Officers and ecosystem partners need a principlesandregulatory compass that is:

  • Normative – grounded in India’s AIforAll vision and global Responsible AI principles.
  • Legal and supervisory – clearly aligned with DPDP obligations and sectoral expectations (RBI, SEBI, IRDAI).
  • Sectorspecific – operationalised through RBI’s FREEAI Sutras and pillars, with explicit relevance to BFSI use cases.

This chapter constructs that compass. Subsequent chapters will apply it to concrete agentic AI workflows—credit, claims, AML, trading, payments—translating abstract principles into design constraints, control patterns and implementation examples.

2.2 Global Responsible AI Principles: The Outer Ring

Globally, Responsible AI frameworks—from OECD, UNESCO and national strategies—converge on a familiar cluster of principles: humancentredness, fairness and nondiscrimination, transparency and explainability, robustness and safety, privacy and security, and accountability.
NITI Aayog’s work explicitly references these norms and emphasises that India’s AI trajectory must embed ethics and trust as enabling conditions for scale, not as afterthefact corrections.

For BFSI, these principles are directly mapped to familiar risk types:

  • Fairness / nondiscrimination → credit and underwriting fairness, nondiscriminatory pricing, avoidance of disparate impact on protected or vulnerable groups.
  • Transparency / explainability → ability to understand and justify decisions in lending, trading, claims and fraud flags, including to customers, regulators and courts.
  • Robustness / safety → resistance to adversarial attacks, model drift, emergent behaviour and cascading failures in agentic workflows.
  • Privacy / security → lawful and safe handling of financial and behavioural data, especially as agentic systems ingest and infer across multiple sources.
  • Accountability → clarity that BFSI institutions remain responsible for AIdriven outcomes, irrespective of vendor, autonomy level, or underlying models.

We will treat this global RAI baseline as the outer ring of the compass. Any agentic AI use case that fundamentally conflicts with these principles—or cannot be rendered consistent with them through design and controls—is outside the scope of responsible deployment, irrespective of local regulatory nuance.

2.3 NITI Aayog’s Responsible AI for All: India’s Normative Anchor

NITI Aayog’s “Responsible AI for All” suite—comprising the initial approach paper, Part 2 on operationalisation, and subsequent work—articulates India’s AI ethics roadmap under the banner of AI for All.
The principles set out in NITI’s documents include: safety and reliability, equality and inclusivity, privacy and security, transparency and accountability, and protection of human dignity and autonomy.

Importantly, NITI moves beyond highlevel ethics to operational guidance. Part 2 emphasises that government, private sector and research institutions each have roles in “operationalizing principles”, recommending:

  • Regulatory and policy interventions that create a trusted AI ecosystem (e.g., standards, oversight, enforcement).
  • Capacitybuilding and awareness efforts across public and private institutions.
  • Alignment of procurement and funding mechanisms with Responsible AI requirements (e.g., ethicsbydesign incentives).
  • Institutional mechanisms—such as expert advisory committees—to vet complex AI projects against RAI principles across development, deployment and scaling stages.

NITI explicitly calls for a “custodian of responsible AI principles” to monitor the environment, update principles and identify mechanisms to translate them into practice.
RBI’s FREEAI Committee, in many respects, operates as such a custodian for the financial sector, interpreting NITI’s nationallevel ethos into BFSIspecific recommendations.

In our compass, NITI’s Responsible AI for All forms the national normative anchor: it defines what “good” looks like for AI in India, including BFSI. We read sectoral regulations and FREEAI through this lens, and we will evaluate agentic AI use cases against their contribution to, or deviation from, AIforAll goals—especially inclusion, safety and humancentricity.

2.4 DPDP Act 2023: Governing Data, Profiling and Automated Decisions

The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDP) is the foundational law for personaldata processing in India, including data used in AI and agentic systems.
DPDP establishes obligations around lawful processing, consent, purpose limitation, data minimisation, accuracy, storage limitation, reasonable security and dataprincipal rights (access, correction, erasure, grievance redress).

Although DPDP does not contain a dedicated article on automated decisionmaking equivalent to GDPR’s Article 22, its general framework fully applies to AIdriven profiling and decisions.

Key aspects for agentic AI in BFSI include:

  • Profiling and automated decisions: any credit score, risk flag, fraud label, dynamic price or claim decision produced by algorithms based on personal data, without meaningful human review, is an automated decision under DPDPaligned interpretations.
  • Transparency in automated processing: privacy notices must disclose that automated processing takes place, the categories of personal data used as inputs, the decisions or outputs generated, and how these may affect individuals.
  • Accuracy and grievance redress: DPDP heightens obligations around data accuracy where decisions significantly affect individuals and allows data principals to challenge decisions by seeking correction and reevaluation.
  • Significant Data Fiduciaries (SDFs): large BFSI entities processing high volumes of personal data—especially with AI—are likely to be classified as SDFs, triggering Data Protection Officer (DPO) appointments, independent data audits, DPIAs and additional safeguards.

From an agentic AI perspective, DPDP is not just a legal backdrop; it is a design constraint on agent memory, data flows and decision autonomy:

  • Agents that continuously ingest, infer and act on personal data require documented purposes, lawful bases and consent flows that are comprehensible to data principals.
  • Persistent memory and logs must respect minimisation and retention, and support access and correction rights (including logs of decisions and input data).
  • Highimpact automated decisions—credit, claims, trading flags—should be subject to DPIAstyle analysis in SDFs, capturing risks of bias, discrimination and systemic harm, and, where appropriate, designing human review or escalation paths.

In our compass, DPDP forms the dataprotection and automateddecision axis. When we later examine agentic AI workflows, we will ask: how is consent obtained and maintained; how are purposes defined; how is profiling disclosed; and what mechanisms exist for access, correction and challenge?

2.5 Sectoral Technology and AI Regimes: Making AI Visible and Governed

India’s financial regulators have progressively built their own technologyrisk and AI visibility regimes, which now intersect directly with agentic AI.

2.5.1 RBI: Cyber Security and IT Directions

RBI’s Cyber Security Framework in Banks and related IT directions require banks and NBFCs to maintain boardapproved policies, robust cyberincident response plans, system security baselines, continuous monitoring, and regular audits of critical systems.
These frameworks explicitly cover all technology infrastructures—including applications, data centres, networks and thirdparty services—and expect banks to treat new technologies (including AI) as part of the controlled IT landscape, not exempt experiments.

For agentic AI, this implies:

  • AI platforms, agents and orchestration layers must be on the risk radar: included in IT inventories, subject to changemanagement, access control, logging, security testing and incidentresponse procedures.
  • Agentic workflows that interact with core banking, payments or treasury systems require architecture reviews and security assessments similar to other missioncritical systems.

2.5.2 SEBI: AI/ML Reporting and System Audit

SEBI’s 2019 circulars introduced mandatory AI/ML reporting and inventory requirements for market infrastructure institutions (MIIs), market intermediaries (brokers, depository participants) and mutual funds/AMCs.

These circulars require covered entities to:

  • File periodic reports (quarterly or halfyearly) listing AI/ML applications and systems, including “NIL” reports where no such systems exist.
  • Disclose application names, firstuse dates, functional areas (e.g., order execution, advisory, KYC, AML, surveillance, compliance), claims made about AI/ML, abnormalbehaviour safeguards, and inclusion of systems in system audits.
  • Ensure AI/ML applications meet SEBImandated cybersecurity control requirements, and are explicitly covered in audit scopes.

SEBI’s AI/ML regime signals two important expectations for agentic AI in capital markets and wealth:

  • AI systems must be visible and inventoried, not hidden inside blackbox vendor offerings.
  • AIdriven workflows affecting trading, advice or surveillance must be auditable, with controls, safeguards and documented behaviour.

2.5.3 IRDAI: Policyholder Protection and Digital Claims

IRDAI’s regulations and communications emphasise fair and transparent treatment of policyholders, including claimsettlement timelines, clarity of communication and dispute resolution.
As insurers adopt mobileappbased, AIassisted claim assessment (e.g., motor damage photos), IRDAI’s expectations imply that:

  • AIassisted claim decisions must remain explainable and contestable; policyholders should have avenues to challenge outcomes and seek human review.
  • AIdriven underwriting and pricing must avoid unjust discrimination, particularly where proxies for protected characteristics may creep into training data or risk models.

In our compass, these sectoral regimes form the technologyrisk and consumerprotection axis. They collectively require that AI and agentic systems be inventoried, audited, secured and aligned with existing obligations on transparency, fairness and conduct.

2.6 FREEAI: Sutras and Pillars as the BFSI Core

RBI’s FREEAI framework is the central BFSIspecific articulation of Responsible AI.
The committee report, building on surveys of over 600 entities and global benchmarks, notes that only about onefifth of surveyed institutions are using or developing AI, with larger banks leading adoption—but anticipates rapid growth and emphasises the need to preemptively shape AI governance.

The framework introduces seven Sutras:

  1. Trust is the Foundation – AI must reinforce, not erode, trust in the financial system.
  2. People First – AI should augment human judgment and protect human interests.
  3. Innovation over Restraint – responsible innovation is preferred to prohibition; sandboxing and guardrails are key.
  4. Fairness and Equity – outcomes should be free from unjust discrimination and bias.
  5. Accountability – financial institutions remain responsible for AI deployment and outcomes.
  6. Understandable by Design – explainability and interpretability must be embedded in AI systems.
  7. Safety, Resilience and Sustainability – AI infrastructures must be secure, resilient and resourceconscious.

These Sutras are operationalised via six strategic pillars:

  • Infrastructure – sectoral data infrastructure, AI testbeds and compute resources as public and shared goods.
  • Policy – institutional and sectoral AI policies, charters and liability frameworks that clarify expectations and responsibilities.
  • Capacity – skills, literacy and organisational capabilities across boards, management, practitioners and regulators.
  • Governance – AI inventories, risk tiering, committees, RACI matrices and oversight mechanisms.
  • Protection – consumer safeguards, grievance redressal, fairness controls and incidenthandling for AIdriven systems.
  • Assurance – validation, audit, monitoring and continuous improvement of AI systems.

The 26 recommendations grouped under these pillars cover topics such as:

  • Establishing financialsector data infrastructure as digital public goods to support trustworthy models.
  • Creating AI innovation sandboxes for safe experimentation and pilots.
  • Mandating AI inventories, risktiering and governance structures at institutional level.
  • Incentivising explainable and fairnessaware AI, and strengthening grievance and redressal mechanisms for AIdriven decisions.
  • Building AI literacy across regulators, institutions and customers, and evolving RegTech/SupTech for supervisory use.

For agentic AI, FREEAI carries three particularly important messages:

  • Autonomy does not absolve institutions of accountability: responsibility for AI outcomes “rests squarely” with deploying institutions.
  • Innovation and risk mitigation must be treated as twin pillars, not sequential phases; agentic AI must be architected with governance, protection and assurance baked in from the start.
  • Humancentricity and explainability are mandatory, not optional, in designing agentic workflows that will affect customers, markets and systemic stability.

We will therefore use the Sutras as the inner normative core of our compass, and the six pillars as the organising spine for our operational guidance in later chapters.

2.7 Putting It Together: The Compass for Agentic AI Design

If we layer these elements, we arrive at a multiaxis compass for BFSI agentic AI:

  • Outer ring – Global RAI principles: fairness, transparency, robustness, privacy, accountability.
  • National anchor – NITI Responsible AI for All: AIforAll ethos, humancentric and inclusive deployment, operationalisation mechanisms.
  • Data axis – DPDP Act: lawful basis, consent and purpose limitation, profiling and automated decisions, SDF obligations, dataprincipal rights.
  • Sectoral axis – RBI/SEBI/IRDAI tech and conduct regimes: cyber security, IT governance, AI/ML reporting and audit, policyholder/investor protection.
  • Core – FREEAI Sutras and pillars: trust, peoplefirst, fairness, accountability, explainability, safety; innovation enablement (Infrastructure, Policy, Capacity) and risk mitigation (Governance, Protection, Assurance).

When we overlay the mixed signals from leaders—excitement about efficiency and innovation; concern about opacity, bias and regulatory exposure—the compass offers a way to structure design tradeoffs:

  • A highimpact agentic AI use case (e.g., endtoend digital lending agent) must be examined through all axes:
  • Does it advance AIforAll goals (broader access, better experience) without deepening exclusion?
  • Does its profiling and automated decisions respect DPDP obligations and rights?
  • Are its data, models and workflows visible and governed under RBI/SEBI/IRDAI regimes?
  • Is it architected to embody FREEAI Sutras and pillars, with clear governance, protection and assurance mechanisms?
  • If leaders are excited about a use case but cannot answer these questions convincingly, that excitement is structurally misaligned with the compass.
  • Conversely, if concerns arise but can be mitigated through the compass (e.g., by adding fairness testing, humanintheloop review, DPDPaligned notices and logs, audit coverage), then agentic AI can be responsibly adopted rather than deferred.

In the chapters that follow, we will apply this compass systematically:

  • Mapping specific agentic AI use cases in banking, securities, insurance and payments to principles, obligations and FREEAI pillars.
  • Identifying where practice today diverges from the compass (based on our primary research).
  • Proposing concrete operational patterns—controls, artefacts, organisational structures—that BFSI institutions, vendors and fintechs can adopt to move their agentic AI programmes into alignment.

Chapter 3 – RBI’s FREEAI Framework as the BFSI Backbone

3.1 Origin and Intent: Why FREEAI Exists

RBI did not approach AI governance as a single circular or checklist; it commissioned a dedicated committee in December 2024 with a clear mandate: assess AI adoption in Indian and global finance, review regulatory approaches, identify risks, and recommend a governance framework for responsible, ethical AI in the financial sector.
The committee’s report, released in August 2025, explicitly positions FREEAI as a principlesbased yet operationally actionable framework, designed to “harness AI’s potential while safeguarding against associated risks,” with a forwardlooking approach organised into seven Sutras and 26 recommendations under six strategic pillars.

FREEAI is therefore not just another compliance artefact. It is RBI’s attempt to encode AI ethics into sectorspecific control objectives—governance, risk management, data stewardship, model lifecycle management, stakeholder engagement and innovation enablement—drawing on consultations with banks, fintechs, academics and technology leaders.
For agentic AI, this matters: the framework is explicitly aware of autonomous AI systems, AI sandboxes, sectorwide data infrastructure, and AI liability, and is structured to shape how BFSI institutions build, deploy and govern increasingly autonomous AI stacks.

3.2 The Seven Sutras, Interpreted for Agentic AI

The core of FREEAI is its seven Sutras, which are intended to guide all AI adoption in the financial sector, irrespective of technology or use case.
For agentic AI, each Sutra translates into specific design and governance implications.

  1. Trust is the Foundation
    Public trust in financial institutions is nonnegotiable; AI systems must reinforce, not erode, confidence in markets and intermediaries.
    For agentic AI, this implies that autonomy must always be bounded by observable behaviour, robust logging and demonstrable controls that can withstand supervisory, audit and judicial scrutiny.
  2. People First
    Individuals should retain “final authority to override AI systems,” and institutions should disclose when AI is used and allow customers to switch to human channels.
    In agentic settings—loan approvals, claims settlement, trading recommendations—this Sutra translates into humanintheloop/ontheloop/incommand patterns, explicit override mechanisms, and the right to request human review.
  3. Innovation over Restraint
    FREEAI consciously avoids a “banfirst” posture; it favours responsible, socially useful innovation over blanket caution.
    For agentic AI, this supports experimentation via sandboxes and controlled pilots, but only when accompanied by risk tiering, guardrails and continuous validation.
  4. Fairness and Equity
    AI systems should be designed and tested to promote fairness, equity and inclusion, and avoid discriminatory outcomes.
    Agentic AI that automates underwriting, pricing or collections must therefore integrate bias audits, protectedattribute awareness and disparateimpact thresholds as routine controls, not optional extras.
  5. Accountability
    Entities deploying AI remain accountable “regardless of the level of autonomy of the systems”; responsibility cannot be outsourced or delegated to vendors.
    For agentic AI, this is explicit: even when agents act autonomously, the regulated entity owns the decision, the incident, and the remediation.
  6. Understandable by Design
    AI systems should be “understandable by design” to deploying entities, fostering trust and enabling informed oversight.
    In agentic contexts, this Sutra demands explainability tooling (model cards, decision traces, SHAP/LIME/counterfactuals) integrated into MLOps, so that engineers, risk and compliance can reconstruct decision paths.
  7. Safety, Resilience and Sustainability
    AI systems must be robust to physical and cyber risks, resilient under stress, and operationally sustainable.
    Agentic AI architectures therefore need kill switches, circuit breakers, businesscontinuity plans, adversarial testing and drift monitoring, treating AI failures as operational risk events with predefined fallbacks.

Taken together, these Sutras define what “responsible autonomy” means: agentic AI can execute, but only within a humananchored, explainable, fair and safe envelope.

3.3 The Six Strategic Pillars and 26 Recommendations: What They Ask BFSI to Build

FREEAI organises its 26 recommendations into six strategic pillars, split across an Innovation Enablement Framework (Infrastructure, Policy, Capacity) and a Risk Mitigation Framework (Governance, Protection, Assurance).

3.3.1 Infrastructure

RBI calls for financialsector data infrastructure—integrated with IndiaAI’s AI Kosh—and AI innovation sandboxes to democratise access to highquality data and safe experimentation, especially for smaller institutions.
For agentic AI, this means building or using shared data platforms and AI testbeds where multiagent workflows can be trialled under synthetic or controlled data, with regulators aware of the sandbox and its safeguards.

Key recommendations include:

  • Sectorwide data infrastructure as a digital public good.
  • AI innovation sandboxes for banks, NBFCs, PSOs and fintechs.
  • Incentives, funding and shared compute for smaller REs to participate.

3.3.2 Policy

Under Policy, FREEAI expects regulators and regulated entities to adopt adaptive, AIspecific policy frameworks, including an AI liability framework and boardapproved institutional AI policies.

At RE level, Rec 14 explicitly mandates a boardapproved AI policy covering:

  • Governance structure and accountability.
  • Risk appetite and classification (low/medium/high, with highrisk examples including credit underwriting and autonomous systems that move funds).
  • Consumerprotection measures, disclosures and grievance mechanisms.
  • Lifecycle management and thirdparty vendor liabilities.

For agentic AI, this policy is the constitutional document: it should define where autonomy is allowed, where human oversight is mandatory, and how AI risk is managed across products.

3.3.3 Capacity

The Capacity pillar emphasises skills, literacy and institutional capability across REs and regulators.
Recommendations include capacitybuilding for boards and staff, establishing an AI institute for training supervisors, sharing best practices, and recognising responsible AI innovation.

For agentic AI, capacity is a hard constraint: if boards, risk committees and technology teams cannot understand or challenge agent behaviour, autonomy becomes ungovernable. FREEAI therefore pushes for:

  • Board and EXCO training on AI risk, fairness, explainability and security.
  • Crossfunctional AI governance committees with real decision power.

3.3.4 Governance

The Governance pillar houses some of the most concrete expectations for agentic AI.

Key recommendations include:

  • AI inventory and repository (Rec 23): every material AI system must be registered, riskclassified, and associated with owners and documentation.
  • AI system governance framework (Rec 16): robust lifecycle governance across design, development, deployment, monitoring and decommissioning, with particularly strong controls for autonomous systems.
  • Product approval process with AIspecific gates (Rec 17): independent review teams must assess fairness, bias, explainability, cybersecurity and compliance before AIenabled products launch.
  • Data lifecycle governance (Rec 15): controls over data quality, lineage, access, retention and deletion for all data used in AI systems.

For agentic AI, governance means hard wiring accountability and lifecycle discipline into MLOps and product pipelines: no agent goes live without being in the inventory, classified, reviewed and assigned owners.

3.3.5 Protection

The Protection pillar translates consumerprotection and security into AIspecific safeguards.

Key elements include:

  • Consumer protection (Rec 18): customers must be explicitly informed when interacting with AI and given the option to switch to human channels; fairness and grievance mechanisms must be in place.
  • Cybersecurity measures: AI systems must be protected against AIspecific threats (prompt injection, data poisoning, model theft), and AI itself can be used for threat detection.
  • Red teaming (Rec 20): medium and highrisk AI applications should undergo regular adversarial testing (e.g., semiannual) plus triggerbased exercises after major updates or incidents.
  • Business continuity plans for AI (Rec 21): models must be able to declare themselves unavailable and trigger deterministic fallback processes; periodic human sample reviews should detect silent degradation.

Agentic AI, by definition, sits in the highrisk space. FREEAI expects redteaming, killswitches, circuit breakers and BCPs to be in place; “alwayson” agents without tested fallback paths are misaligned with this pillar.

3.3.6 Assurance

Finally, the Assurance pillar covers validation, audit, monitoring and compliance toolkits.

Recommendations include:

  • AI audit framework: riskbased internal and external audits of AI systems, including fairness, performance, robustness and security.
  • Public disclosures (Rec 25): AI governance and usage disclosures in annual reports and on websites, including adoption areas and consumerprotection measures.
  • AI compliance toolkit: standardised tools to help REs assess and demonstrate responsible AI use.

For agentic AI, assurance becomes continuous: drift monitoring, fairness metrics, stability checks, incident logs and redteam results should feed into audit and board reporting, creating a single system of record for AI risk.

3.4 FREEAI and Agentic AI: Autonomy, Accountability and Human Oversight

FREEAI is explicit about human oversight patterns. Commentary and derivative guidance from practitioners emphasise three modes: humanintheloop (HITL), humanontheloop (HOTL), and humanincommand, depending on risk level.
Rec 16 (AI system governance) and Rec 21 (BCP for AI) imply that autonomous tasks must be clearly defined, with documented boundaries and escalation paths, and that human review remains mandatory for highrisk decisions.

For agentic AI in BFSI, this leads to concrete design imperatives:

  • Risktiered autonomy: lowrisk tasks (e.g., document extraction, appointment scheduling) can be fully automated; mediumrisk tasks (e.g., initial credit rule checks) require HOTL with sampled human validation; highrisk tasks (e.g., loan denial, claim repudiation, highvalue trades) demand HITL, explicit human signoff or posthoc human sampling with override capacity.
  • Kill switch and circuit breaker mechanisms: any agent controlling financial actions should have technical controls to halt operations when thresholds are breached or anomalies detected, and these events must be logged as operational incidents.
  • Endtoend audit trail: actions taken by agents must be traced to inputs, models, configurations and approvals, so that institutions can reconstruct decision chains and demonstrate accountability.

FREEAI thus reframes autonomy: agentic AI is acceptable, but only in a humanstructured governance envelope where autonomy is always answerable to accountable humans and institutions.

3.5 FREEAI and AI Model Risk Management

Although FREEAI is not branded as a “model risk management” (MRM) standard, it maps naturally onto emerging AIMRM practices in Indian banking.
Industry interpretations and tool vendors align FREEAI with a registerclassifyvalidatemonitorreport cycle, particularly for medium and highrisk models, including agentic systems.

Typical FREEAIaligned MRM stack elements include:

  • AI inventory / MRM vault: a single source of truth for all AI models and agentic systems, satisfying Rec 23 and forming the foundation for governance, validation and reporting.
  • Validation and testing engines: automated bias, drift and performance testing, integrated with redming and regression test suites to operationalise Protection and Assurance pillars.
  • Model cards and documentation: standardised artefacts covering purpose, inputs, training data, evaluation metrics, fairness tests, limitations and change history, feeding audit and disclosure requirements.
  • Incident and killswitch logs: structured records of AI incidents, killswitch triggers, fallbacks and remediation actions, supporting incident reporting and continuous improvement.

For agentic AI, this stack needs to handle not just models but multiagent workflows: the inventory must register composite systems; validation must consider emergent behaviour; and assurances must cover orchestration logic, not only individual models.

3.6 What FREEAI Expects BFSI Technologists and CAIOs to Do

From a technologist / CAIO perspective, FREEAI translates into a concrete build agenda rather than a purely policy document. A pragmatic reading suggests at least five nearterm tasks:

  1. Establish a boardapproved AI policy and governance structure
  2. Draft and obtain approval for an AI policy that explicitly covers agentic AI, risk tiering, autonomy boundaries, HITL/HOTL patterns, vendor obligations and lifecycle controls.
  3. Constitute or strengthen an AI governance committee at board or EXCO level, with clear RACI across business, risk, compliance, IT and data.
  4. Build and maintain a comprehensive AI inventory
  5. Register all material models and agentic systems, including vendorprovided tools, with risk classification and ownership, aligned to Rec 23.
  6. Ensure inventories are integrated into architecture and changemanagement processes, not kept in isolated spreadsheets.
  7. Embed FREEAI pillars into MLOps and product pipelines
  8. Incorporate fairness testing, explainability, security review and DPDPaligned data checks into CI/CD pipelines for AI and agentic workflows.
  9. Add AIspecific gates to product approvals (Rec 17), with independent review teams assessing usecases before launch.
  10. Operationalise Protection and Assurance for agentic AI
  11. Design and implement killswitches, fallback processes, redteaming schedules, monitoring dashboards and periodic human sampling for medium and highrisk agentic workflows.
  12. Set up structured incidentreporting channels and AIaware BCPs, treating AI failures as distinct operational risk events.
  13. Prepare for disclosures and supervisory dialogue
  14. Develop AI governance and usage disclosures for annual reports and websites (Rec 25), including highlevel descriptions of agentic AI use and consumer safeguards.
  15. Engage early with regulators and industry bodies on implementing AI sandboxes, sectoral data infrastructure and bestpractice sharing under FREEAI and NITI RA.

Chapter 4 – Operationalising RAI Across the AI Lifecycle

4.1 From Principles to Pipelines: Why Lifecycle Discipline Is the Real Test of RAI

Chapters 2 and 3 established the compass—NITI’s AIforAll ethos, DPDP’s data obligations, sectoral technology regimes, and FREEAI’s seven Sutras and six pillars. The genuinely hard part of Responsible AI, however, is not articulating principles; it is embedding them into the engineering and governance mechanics of the AI lifecycle, so that fairness, explainability, safety and accountability survive contact with production systems, legacy integrations and commercial pressure to ship fast.

This imperative has recently acquired unusual regulatory precision. On 24 June 2026, RBI released its draft Guidance on Regulatory Principles for Model Risk Management (MRM), 2026, open for public comment until 24 July 2026. This draft is best read as the operational sequel to FREEAI: where FREEAI set out Sutras and strategic pillars, the MRM draft prescribes the actual lifecycle mechanics—inventory, tiering, validation, deployment controls, human oversight, change management and decommissioning—that regulated entities (REs) must apply to “any quantitative, algorithmic, analytical or judgemental technique with input, processing and output components used in business operations and decisionmaking, whether or not the RE recognises it as a model.” Critically, this definition is written broadly enough to capture agentic AI systems, foundational and frontier AI models, and thirdparty/embedded AI—not just classical statistical credit models.

This chapter therefore treats the AI/ML lifecycle as a sixstage regulated pipeline—Selection & Development, Validation, Approval, Deployment & Monitoring, Change Management, and Continuity & Decommissioning—and shows, stage by stage, how BFSI technologists and CAIOs can operationalise RAI principles (fairness, explainability, safety, accountability, privacy) using controls anchored in FREEAI, the MRM draft, NITI RA and DPDP, illustrated with concrete agentic AI examples.

4.2 Stage 0 – Model/Agent Discovery and Risk Tiering (Before the Lifecycle Begins)

Before any lifecycle stage can be meaningfully governed, an institution must know what it actually has. The MRM draft is unambiguous: “No model may be used, relied upon or deployed unless it is part of the inventory,” with minimum fields covering owners, developers, validators, approvers, risk tier, intended use, and upstream/downstream dependencies. This directly operationalises FREEAI Recommendation 23 (AI inventory and repository).

Discovery is harder than it sounds for agentic AI. Traditional model inventories were built around discrete, named statistical models owned by a quant team. Agentic systems, by contrast, are often:

  • Embedded inside vendor SaaS (a CRM’s “smart assist” feature, a collections platform’s autonegotiation agent) where the RE may not even recognise it is “using a model” — precisely the loophole the MRM draft closes by stating the definition applies “irrespective of whether the RE itself recognises the tool as a model.”
  • Composite and multiagent — a loanorigination workflow might chain a documentextraction agent, a bureaupull agent, a policyrules agent and a customercommunication agent, each potentially sourced from different vendors, with the “model” being the orchestration logic itself.
  • Dynamically selfupdating — GenAIbased agents built on foundation models that vendors update without RE control, which the MRM draft flags explicitly as a distinct risk requiring “strict justifications for enabling automatic updates” and “enhanced data quality checks.”

Operational practice: Institutions should run a structured model/agent discovery sweep across every business function using the RBI’s broad definition — not the narrower definition sitting in the current risk register — asking three questions of every system: (1) does it take input, process it algorithmically/statistically/cognitively, and produce an output used in decisionmaking; (2) does it exercise any degree of autonomy over actions or customerfacing communication; (3) is it sourced, embedded, or selfupdating from a third party. Anything answering yes to (1) enters the inventory before a governance framework is even built.

Risk tiering then follows, based on three MRMdraft criteria — Materiality (business, financial, operational and consumer impact), Complexity (unstructured data use, explainability difficulty, oversight difficulty) and Other factors (regulatory/supervisory sensitivity) — with an explicit fourth dimension added for AI: the extent of reliance and level of autonomy placed on the model’s output for decisionmaking (Para 52). This autonomyweighted tiering is the single most important adaptation for agentic AI: a creditscoring model that merely informs an underwriter’s judgment may sit at a lower tier than an agent of identical predictive accuracy that is permitted to autoapprove or autodecline without human review.

4.3 Stage 1 – Selection & Development: Designing Fairness and Explainability In, Not Bolting Them On

The MRM draft requires that, at development, institutions “document rationale and objectives, scope of application, and costs and benefits — including additional risks, likelihood of adverse outcomes, fairness, ethical considerations and bias,” and follow a structured process across data collection, preprocessing, assumptions/limitations assessment, design, and evaluation/refinement. This is the operational expression of FREEAI Sutra 4 (Fairness and Equity) and Sutra 6 (Understandable by Design).

Fairness engineering. For highimpact use cases — credit underwriting, insurance pricing, collections prioritisation — fairness testing should be a firstclass development deliverable, not a posthoc audit. Established techniques include:

  • Adverse Impact Ratio (AIR): the ratio of the favourableoutcome rate for a protected/vulnerable group to that of a reference group (e.g., approval rate for one demographic segment ÷ approval rate for another); values below conventional thresholds (commonly cited around 0.80) flag potential disparate impact requiring further investigation.
  • Standardised Mean Difference (SMD): used to test for pricing disparities between groups, complementing AIR’s focus on approval/denial decisions.
  • Tradeoff transparency: fairness metrics (AIR/SMD) should be reported alongside accuracy metrics (e.g., AUC) so that model owners and validators can see explicitly where predictiveness and equity diverge, and make a documented, riskcommitteevisible choice rather than an implicit one buried in a hyperparameter.

Explainability engineering. The MRM draft defines explainability as “the extent to which a model’s design, functioning and outputs can be understood, interpreted and communicated” — and explicitly lists it as one of seven AIspecific risk dimensions requiring defined safeguards. For agentic AI, explainability has two layers that must both be engineered:

  1. Modellevel explainability — posthoc techniques such as SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations), LIME (Local Interpretable Modelagnostic Explanations), and counterfactual explanations (“you would have been approved if X had been Y”), applied to the underlying scoring/classification components of the agent.
  2. Agentlevel traceability — because agentic workflows chain multiple decisions and tool calls, explainability must also cover why the agent took the sequence of actions it did: which subagent triggered which API call, what data it retrieved, and why it escalated (or failed to escalate) to a human. This is closer to a “decision trace” or audit log than a classical featureattribution chart, and it is this agentlevel trace that regulators and internal auditors will actually need when reconstructing an adverse outcome.

Data governance at the development stage. The MRM draft requires that “data (empirical or synthetic) must conform to data governance processes,” and separately flags data lineage as essential given AI/ML models’ reliance on “large volumes of heterogeneous and highdimensional data.” This is where DPDP obligations bite directly into engineering practice: development teams must be able to trace training and inference data back to a lawful basis and documented purpose, apply minimisation (using only the fields actually predictive and necessary), and ensure retention schedules for training datasets and derived embeddings are DPDPcompliant rather than indefinite by default.

Practitioner checklist for Selection & Development:

  • Document business rationale, scope and costbenefit including fairness/bias risk, before writing the first line of code.
  • Run AIR/SMD (or equivalent) fairness testing on any protectedattributecorrelated use case, with results reviewed jointly by data science and risk/compliance.
  • Select an explainability toolkit (SHAP/LIME/counterfactuals) matched to model type, and design agentlevel decision logging from day one — not as a retrofit.
  • Establish data lineage and DPDPaligned lawfulbasis documentation for every dataset feeding the model or agent.

4.4 Stage 2 – Validation: Independent Challenge Before Trust Is Extended

The MRM draft mandates independent validation by the RE itself for all models, including thirdparty models — vendor certification is explicitly stated as insufficient. Validation must occur prior to deployment, after deployment, after modification, on internal or external triggers, and periodically thereafter, assessing inputs (data, assumptions, limitations), conceptual and design soundness, performance, and alignment with intended use — closely mirroring the SR 117 “conceptual soundness + outcome analysis + ongoing monitoring” triad used internationally.

For agentic AI specifically, validation must extend beyond the model to the orchestration layer:

  • Conceptual soundness of the agent architecture — does the sequencing of subagents, tool calls and escalation logic match the documented intended use? Are failure modes (e.g., an agent retrying a failed API call indefinitely, or two agents producing contradictory outputs) identified and mitigated?
  • Outcome analysis under adversarial and edgecase conditions — redteaming for prompt injection, jailbreaking, data poisoning and adversarial examples, which the MRM draft and FREEAI both flag as necessary, particularly for customerfacing generative/agentic systems.
  • Hallucination controls for GenAIbased agents — the MRM draft explicitly lists hallucination as a defined AI risk dimension requiring documented safeguards (e.g., retrievalaugmentation grounding, output verification layers, confidence thresholds below which the agent must escalate rather than act).
  • Bias and proxydiscrimination testing at validation, independent of whatever testing development already performed — the second line must be able to reproduce or challenge the first line’s fairness claims, not simply accept them.

Validation reports must be placed before the Risk Management Committee of the Board (RMCB) or delegated authority within three months of completion — a hard timeline that operationalises accountability and prevents validation from becoming a perpetual, unresolved backlog item.

Thirdparty and supplychain risk deserves particular emphasis for agentic AI, since most institutions will source foundation models, orchestration frameworks or specialised agents from a small number of providers. The MRM draft (Para 53) requires REs to separately assess supplychain risk from dependence on a limited number of model providers, limitations in independently validating closedsource models, and the risk that providerdriven updates silently change model behaviour without the RE’s control or consent. Practically, this means validation must include contractual audit rights, access to sufficient documentation to perform meaningful independent testing (not just vendor marketing collateral), and a defined process for revalidating whenever the vendor pushes an update — which for many commercial LLM APIs may be more frequent than annual modelrisk cycles anticipate.

Practitioner checklist for Validation:

  • Constitute an independent validation function (second line) with explicit authority to challenge, and if necessary block, deployment — reporting lines must not run through the model’s business sponsor.
  • Build validation test suites covering conceptual soundness, outcome/bias analysis, hallucination and adversarial robustness, calibrated to the model’s risk tier.
  • For thirdparty/foundationmodelbased agents, negotiate audit rights and documentation access at contracting stage, not after golive.
  • Route all validation reports to RMCB/delegated authority within the threemonth regulatory window, with a documented approve/conditionallyapprove/reject decision.

4.5 Stage 3 – Approval: Gated, RiskProportionate SignOff

FREEAI Recommendation 17 calls for AIspecific gates in the product approval process, with independent review teams assessing fairness, bias, explainability, cybersecurity and compliance before launch. The MRM draft formalises this into a structured approval regime with defined authorities and thresholds, distinguishing normal approvals from exception approvals (with documented rationale and remediation timelines for the latter), and mandating that high or equivalentrisk models require RMCB approval, while lowertier models may follow delegated authority.

For agentic AI, the approval gate should explicitly interrogate:

  • Autonomy boundary signoff — precisely which actions the agent is authorised to take without human confirmation, and which require humaninthe‑loop signoff, documented as an explicit, auditable boundary rather than left to implicit code behaviour.
  • Consumerprotection signoff — confirmation that customerfacing agentic systems disclose AI involvement, offer a route to human assistance on request, and provide an appeal mechanism for AIdriven decisions, consistent with FREEAI Sutra 2 (People First) and Recommendation 18.
  • DPDP signoff — confirmation that consent flows, purpose limitation and automateddecision disclosures required under DPDP have been implemented and tested, not merely drafted.

Practitioner checklist for Approval:

  • Map every agentic use case to a named approval authority based on its risk tier, with RMCB signoff mandatory for hightier autonomous systems.
  • Require an explicit, written “autonomy boundary statement” as part of the approval package.
  • Treat exception approvals (e.g., launching before full fairness testing is complete) as formally tracked technical debt with a remediation deadline, not silent risk acceptance.

4.6 Stage 4 – Deployment and Continuous Monitoring: Confidence Is Never a OneTime Event

The MRM draft requires that deployment be coordinated with IT and data functions, that model outputs be confirmed stable and replicable in production before golive, and that ongoing monitoring be maintained for all deployed models — including thirdparty ones — with enhanced RMCB monitoring for anything approved with exceptions. McKinsey’s 2026 analysis of MRM in the AI era makes the underlying shift explicit: “confidence must be continuously established through realtime postdeployment monitoring, complemented by pointintime validations and escalation mechanisms to manage residual risk” — validation is no longer a static, prelaunch event but an ongoing, usecasedriven discipline embedded across the lifecycle.

For agentic AI, continuous monitoring must track several distinct signal types simultaneously:

  • Statistical drift — concept drift (the relationship between inputs and outcomes changing) and distribution shift (the population of inputs changing), both of which can silently degrade an agent’s decisioning quality without any visible error.
  • Fairness drift — periodic recomputation of AIR/SMD-style metrics in production, since a model validated as fair at launch can drift into disparate impact as the underlying customer population or macroeconomic conditions change.
  • Hallucination and anomalousoutput rates for GenAIbased agents, tracked as a firstclass production metric alongside traditional accuracy.
  • Override and escalation rates — how often human reviewers override the agent’s recommendation, and in what direction; a rising override rate is often the earliest leading indicator of model or agent degradation, well before aggregate accuracy metrics move.
  • Security telemetry — anomalous usage patterns, promptinjection attempts and APIabuse signals for customerfacing or externallyintegrated agents, which the MRM draft requires as part of AI deployment safeguards alongside access controls and integrationpipeline security.

Deployment security controls specified in the draft include access controls to prevent unauthorised access, use or modification; safeguards against cyber risk; and controls for risks arising from external interfaces, APIs or integration pipelines with thirdparty components — all directly relevant to agentic systems, which by design integrate extensively with external tools and data sources. Customer or externallyfacing GenAI/agentic models additionally require limitations on session and context persistence and detection of anomalous usage patterns, guarding against promptinjectiondriven data exfiltration or unauthorised actiontaking.

Industry practice is increasingly using agentic automation itself to scale monitoring — for example, automating the endtoend validation and monitoring pipeline (data extraction → test execution → report generation) so that human validators focus their judgment on flagged exceptions rather than routine checks — provided this automation itself is documented, validated and does not become an unmonitored “model watching models” blind spot.

Practitioner checklist for Deployment & Monitoring:

  • Build a live dashboard tracking drift, fairness metrics, hallucination rate, override rate and security telemetry for every high and mediumtier agent, with defined escalation thresholds.
  • Confirm production output stability against development/validation expectations before golive, and log the confirmation.
  • Apply enhanced monitoring cadence to any model approved with exceptions.
  • Implement session/contextpersistence limits and anomaly detection specifically for customerfacing agentic systems.

4.7 Stage 5 – Human Oversight: The NonNegotiable Control Layer for Agentic Systems

Human oversight receives dedicated, detailed treatment in the MRM draft — Chapter VB.3 — reflecting its centrality to responsible agentic AI. Required mechanisms include:

  • Humanincommand arrangements, encompassing humanintheloop (HITL — a human must approve before an action is taken), humanontheloop (HOTL — a human monitors and can intervene, but the agent acts by default) and other equivalent oversight patterns matched to risk tier.
  • Override, suspension and deactivation mechanisms, explicitly including killswitch arrangements for AI models — a hard technical capability to halt an agent’s operation immediately, distinct from a graceful degradation path.
  • Periodic human review of model outputs and modeldriven decisions to identify anomalies, even where the agent operates predominantly autonomously — i.e., samplingbased human audit as a standing control, not merely an incidenttriggered one.

This directly operationalises FREEAI Sutra 2 (People First) — the principle that individuals should retain final authority to override AI systems — translating it from an ethical commitment into an engineering requirement with named mechanisms. The MRM draft further requires that oversight staff have genuine authority to challenge and stop models, not merely a dashboard view — an important distinction, since many early agentic deployments create monitoring visibility without any real intervention power.

Risktiered oversight design for agentic AI should follow a clear escalation ladder:

  • Lowrisk, highvolume tasks (document extraction, appointment scheduling, FAQ responses): full automation with HOTL sampling review.
  • Mediumrisk tasks (collections negotiation within preapproved parameters, KYC risk scoring): HOTL with mandatory human signoff above a defined threshold (e.g., waivers above a certain amount, or risk scores in an ambiguous band).
  • Highrisk tasks (credit decisioning, claim repudiation, highvalue trade execution, fund movement): HITL as the default, with the agent generating a recommendation and audit trail but a named human required to authorise the final action, plus killswitch availability at all times.

Practitioner checklist for Human Oversight:

  • Document, per agentic use case, exactly which HITL/HOTL pattern applies and why, tied to the risk tier assigned in Stage 0.
  • Build and test killswitch functionality as a releaseblocking requirement for medium and hightier agents — not an afterthought feature.
  • Assign oversight staff explicit, boardrecognised authority to halt or override agent behaviour, and track how often that authority is exercised.
  • Run periodic (not only incidenttriggered) human sampling reviews of agent decisions, feeding findings back into the monitoring dashboard from Stage 4.

4.8 Stage 6 – Change Management: Governing the Moving Target

Unlike traditional statistical models that are relatively static between formal redevelopment cycles, agentic AI systems — particularly those built on foundation models — change continuously through vendor updates, prompt/policy adjustments, and reinforcementstyle finetuning. The MRM draft addresses this directly (Para 56), requiring enhanced controls for models with dynamic or automatic updates: defining the clear scope of what can be automatically updated, strict justification for enabling automatic updates at all, enhanced dataquality checks, and more stringent, more frequent monitoring.

Core changemanagement requirements include defined roles for making and approving changes, controlled enterpriselevel implementation with tested recovery from failed changes, documented impact assessment before any change, a versioned change log with approvals, and — critically — a defined “material change” threshold that automatically retriggers full validation and approval, rather than being left to casebycase judgment.

For agentic AI, a practical materialchange threshold should explicitly capture:

  • Any change to the agent’s autonomy boundary (expanding what it can do without human signoff).
  • Any underlying foundationmodel version upgrade from the vendor, even where the RE did not initiate it.
  • Any change to the tools/APIs the agent is permitted to call, or the systems it can write to.
  • Any material shift in fairness or drift metrics observed in monitoring (Stage 4), which should itself trigger a changemanagement review even absent a deliberate code change.

Enhanced documentation is mandated specifically for AI models “considering their complexity, selfadapting nature, and heavy reliance on training data — to enable traceability, reproducibility, and auditability” — a requirement that should be read as extending to full agent decision logs, not just model cards.

Practitioner checklist for Change Management:

  • Define, in writing, what constitutes a “material change” for each agentic system, explicitly including vendordriven foundationmodel updates.
  • Maintain a versioned change log linking every change to an impact assessment and an approval record.
  • Treat any material change trigger as an automatic reentry into Stage 2 (Validation), not an optional fasttrack.

4.9 Stage 7 – Continuity and Decommissioning: Planning for the Agent’s Failure and Retirement

Model continuity must form part of the institution’s Business Continuity Plan (BCP), covering scenarios of unavailability, performance degradation and outright failure, with defined fallback mechanisms — manual intervention, substitution with a backup model, or reversion to a simpler rulesbased process. For an agent embedded deep inside a customer journey (e.g., an autonomous claimssettlement agent), the fallback plan must specify precisely how a claim in flight is handed to a human without customervisible disruption — this is materially harder to design retroactively than at architecture time, reinforcing why continuity planning belongs in Stage 1, not as a Stage 7 afterthought.

On decommissioning, the draft requires timely stakeholder communication and — notably — retention of decommissioned models in the inventory for at least ten years from decommissioning, or from the date they cease to serve as a backup/benchmark reference, whichever is later (or longer if other law requires). This tenyear retention is a significant operational commitment for agentic systems built on rapidly iterating vendor platforms, where documentation may otherwise be lost as vendors deprecate older model versions — institutions should proactively archive vendor documentation, prompts, configuration and evaluation results at the point of decommissioning, since these may not remain retrievable from the vendor itself a decade later.

Practitioner checklist for Continuity & Decommissioning:

  • Design and test humanhandoff fallback paths for every hightier agent as part of initial architecture, not as a later BCP exercise.
  • Archive complete decommissioning documentation (including thirdparty/vendor artefacts) for the mandated tenyear retention window at the point of retirement, anticipating vendorside data loss.

4.10 Consumer Protection and Grievance Redressal: The Thread Running Through Every Stage

Two consumerfacing obligations cut across the entire lifecycle rather than sitting in a single stage. First, the MRM draft states plainly that “an RE must not use any model that harms consumers,” and requires the grievance redressal mechanism to explicitly address grievances arising from consumerfacing models — meaning a customer must be able to identify that an AI/agentic system was involved in a decision affecting them and route a complaint accordingly, not be left contesting an opaque “system decision.” Second, customerfacing AI must disclose itself and offer a human option on request — a direct, enforceable expression of FREEAI Sutra 2 (People First) and Recommendation 18.

For agentic AI, operationalising this means: (a) every customerfacing agent interaction carries a clear, accessible disclosure that AI is involved; (b) a persistent, lowfriction pathway to a human agent exists at every step, not buried three menus deep; (c) the grievance system is wired to the agent’s decision log (Stage 1/4 outputs), so that a complaint can be resolved by reconstructing exactly what the agent did and why, within the timelines expected under sectoral conduct regulations.

4.11 Synthesising the Lifecycle: A Worked Example — Autonomous Digital Lending Agent

To make this concrete, consider a hightier autonomous digitallending agent that collects applications conversationally, extracts data from bank statements, pulls a bureau report, applies policy rules and generates a credit decision, escalating only exceptions to a human underwriter.

  • Stage 0: Registered in the AI inventory as a composite, highautonomy, highmateriality system; tiered High given its financial impact and decisioning autonomy (Para 52).
  • Stage 1: Development documents fairness rationale, runs AIR testing across gender/geography proxies, builds SHAPbased feature attribution and a full agent decisiontrace logger; training data lineage mapped to DPDPlawful consented sources.
  • Stage 2: Independent validation reproduces fairness tests, redteams for adversarial document manipulation, tests hallucinationfree bureaudata interpretation, and reviews vendor bureauAPI dependency as a supplychain risk; report to RMCB within three months.
  • Stage 3: RMCB approval with an explicit autonomy boundary statement — autoapprove up to a defined ticket size and risk band; all declines and abovethreshold approvals routed to human underwriter (HITL).
  • Stage 4: Live dashboard tracks approvalrate drift by segment, override rate by underwriters, and bureauAPI anomaly signals; enhanced monitoring given exceptionbased launch conditions.
  • Stage 5: Killswitch tested and available to risk officers; periodic human sampling of 5% of autoapproved decisions monthly.
  • Stage 6: Any bureauAPI version change or policyrule update above a defined threshold triggers automatic revalidation.
  • Stage 7: BCP defines manual underwriting fallback if the agent or bureau API becomes unavailable; decommissioning plan commits to tenyear documentation retention.
  • Crosscutting: Customer sees a disclosure that an AI system assisted their application, with a persistent “talk to a human” option and a grievance channel tied to the decision log.

This worked example illustrates the central argument of this chapter: Responsible AI in agentic BFSI systems is not a separate governance overlay — it is the lifecycle itself, executed with discipline, using FREEAI’s Sutras as design intent and RBI’s emerging Model Risk Management regime as the concrete control architecture that makes that intent auditable, defensible and durable.

Chapter 5 – RAI Governance and Organisational Design

5.1 Why Governance, Not Technology, Is the Real Bottleneck for Agentic AI

Every prior chapter has shown a consistent pattern: FREEAI's Sutras, RBI's draft Model Risk Management guidance, and the AI lifecycle itself all converge on one message — autonomy does not dilute accountability. An agent that acts on its own does not become "the model's decision"; it remains, unambiguously, the regulated entity's decision. This principle has direct organisational consequences: an institution cannot deploy agentic AI safely without first answering who decides, who is accountable, who can override, and who reports to the board — and answering these questions requires deliberate governance design, not an implicit assumption that "the tech team will handle it."

This chapter builds the organisational architecture that operationalises everything covered so far — translating FREEAI Recommendation 14 (BoardApproved AI Policy), Recommendation 16 (AI System Governance Framework) and Recommendation 23 (AI Inventory) into concrete committee structures, roles, RACI matrices and vendorgovernance practices that a BFSI institution, or a fintech serving one, can actually stand up.

5.2 The BoardApproved AI Policy: The Constitutional Document

FREEAI Recommendation 14 requires every regulated entity to have a boardapproved AI policy covering governance structure, accountability, risk appetite, operational safeguards, auditability, consumerprotection measures, AI disclosures, the model lifecycle framework and a liability framework. This is not a generic "AI ethics statement" — RBI's committee report is explicit that the policy must include a risk classification framework categorising AI use cases as low/medium/high based on customer impact, criticality and potential for harm, with credit underwriting, autonomous financial decisioning and systems that move customer funds named explicitly as highrisk exemplars.

What the policy must actually contain (drawing directly from the FREEAI text and its governancestructure annexure):

  • Governance structure and roles — who decides, who has oversight authority, and what the escalation path looks like when something goes wrong.
  • Grievance redressal mechanism — an explicit channel through which customers affected by AIdriven decisions can complain and get resolution, wired into the institution's existing grievance infrastructure rather than a parallel, hardertofind channel.
  • Risk appetite statement for AI — a boardlevel articulation of what kinds of AI risk the institution is willing to accept (e.g., piloting agentic collections at modest ticket sizes) versus what it will not accept without additional safeguards (e.g., fully autonomous claim repudiation).
  • Liability framework — clarity on how the institution allocates responsibility internally (business owner vs. technology vs. risk) and externally (vendor contracts), consistent with the principle that liability for outcomes cannot be contracted away.

Practical guidance: Treat the AI policy as a living document reviewed at least annually, or immediately after any material AI incident, new highrisk use case, or regulatory update (such as the 2026 MRM draft). Industry bodies are expected to help smaller entities with policy templates — smaller BFSI players and fintechs should actively seek and adapt these rather than drafting from a blank page.

5.3 Governance Structures: From Board to Front Line

5.3.1 Board and Board Risk Committee

The board (or its designated Risk Management Committee, RMCB) sits at the apex of AI governance, with three concrete responsibilities under the emerging regime: approving the AI policy, approving or reviewing highrisk AI use cases and validation reports (within the threemonth window the MRM draft specifies), and receiving quarterly disclosures on the AI inventory, material incidents and fairlending/biastesting results. Deloitte's 2026 guidance to midmarket banks is blunt on this point: AI risk remains the bank's risk regardless of vendor involvement, and the board cannot discharge its oversight duty by simply trusting that "the vendor has certified it."

5.3.2 The AI/RAI Steering Committee (Management Level)

Below the board, most mature governance models establish a crossfunctional AI or RAI steering committee — sometimes called a steering committee, advisory forum or strategy council — bringing together privacy, legal, compliance, cybersecurity, enterprise architecture, modelrisk management, technology and business representatives. This committee's job is to own the deployment gate: every AI use case, and especially every agentic one, passes through this committee before golive, ensuring a consistent governance check rather than a fragmented, adhoc signoff scattered across silos.

A pragmatic structure, drawn from converging industry practice, looks like this:

Layer Role Core Responsibility
Board / RMCB Ultimate oversight Approves AI policy; signoff on highrisk use cases; receives quarterly AI risk disclosures
AI Steering Committee Crossfunctional gate Chairs deployment decisions; maintains enterprisewide AI risk view; escalates material issues to RMCB
Chief AI Officer (CAIO) / Head of AI Strategy + governance ownership Owns AI roadmap, chairs/cochairs steering committee, sets vendor duediligence bar, presents to board quarterly

This maps directly onto the classic Three Lines of Defence model that banking organisations already use for enterprise risk management — the innovation for agentic AI is simply ensuring AI/agentic systems are explicitly in scope of each line, rather than treated as a novel category that falls outside existing risk architecture.

5.3.3 The Chief AI Officer: A Role Taking Shape

Across global banking, the Chief AI Officer (CAIO) role has emerged specifically to own this crosscutting accountability — typically reporting to the Group CEO, COO or CIO, with direct oversight of AI governance, data science, AI engineering and often regional AI leads. Job specifications for this role converge on four pillars: (1) enterprise AI strategy and roadmap prioritisation calibrated to what the organisation can actually govern, not just what it can build; (2) chairing the crossfunctional governance committee; (3) owning vendor and thirdparty AI oversight at the strategic level, setting duediligence bars before commercial conversations even begin; and (4) presenting a consolidated AI risk view — inventory, material changes, biastesting results, incidents — to the board risk committee on a quarterly cadence.

A critical, oftenoverlooked design point: the CAIO's reporting line must sit high enough to give genuine authority to say no to an ungoverned deployment — a CAIO who reports several layers below the business heads whose projects they must gate will structurally lack the standing to block a revenuegenerating agentic AI launch that hasn't cleared validation.

5.4 RACI for Agentic AI: Who Does What, Precisely

Ambiguity about "who owns the agent" is one of the most common failure modes observed in early agentic AI deployments — an agent spans business, technology, data science and risk, and without an explicit RACI, each function assumes another owns the risk. A worked RACI for a typical highrisk agentic use case (e.g., an autonomous creditdecisioning agent) should look like this:

This is deliberately explicit because RBI's committee report treats grievance redressal, escalation and decisionmaking accountability as governancestructure requirements in their own right, not implicit outcomes of "good management."

5.5 ThirdParty and Vendor Governance: Where Agentic AI Risk Actually Concentrates

A recent industry survey finding is worth foregrounding here: 72% of banks reportedly do not know which of their vendors use AI at all — meaning the single largest agentic AI governance gap in most institutions is not their inhouse models, but AI quietly embedded inside vendor platforms they have already procured for other reasons (CRM, collections, document processing, chat). This is precisely the blind spot RBI's broad model definition — "whether or not the RE recognises it as a model" — is designed to close.

A practical vendor AI governance programme should run through five domains, drawing directly from current thirdparty AI risk practice:

  1. Vendor AI governance program — request the vendor's written AI governance policy, confirm named accountable owners, request a model inventory reference for exactly which model/version serves your use case, and request independent attestation (e.g., ISO/IEC 42001) as an upstream input — never as a substitute for the institution's own validation.
  2. Data handling and trainingdata provenance — verify in writing whether the institution's prompts, documents and customer data are used to train or improve the vendor's models, and secure a notrain commitment where data is sensitive; confirm data residency, retention and deletion terms.
  3. Model transparency and documentation — request intended use, known limitations, evaluation results and version identifiers; confirm whether the underlying foundation model is firstparty or sourced further upstream (a fourthparty dependency); request biastesting evidence specifically for credit, pricing or underwriting use cases.
  4. AIspecific security testing — request evidence of redteaming against the deployed model (not merely a generic penetration test of the surrounding application), and confirm testing against prompt injection, jailbreaks and the OWASP Top 10 for LLM applications.
  5. Change and incident notification — contractually require notification of material AI/model changes (including silent vendorside updates) and audit rights, with drift monitoring as an ongoingmonitoring obligation on the institution's side regardless of what the vendor promises.

Tiered vendor risk classification should mirror the internal modelrisk tiering from Chapter 4, using a simple, fast disclosure request rather than burying AI questions inside an annual, generic duediligence questionnaire:

Vendor AI Tier Description Assessment Cadence Example
Tier 1 — Direct impact AI directly affects customerfacing decisions Quarterly review + annual deep assessment Credit decisioning, fraud detection, automated underwriting agents
Tier 2 — Indirect impact AI used internally but influences outputs received Semiannual review Documentprocessing agents, analytics platforms, chatbots
Tier 3 — AIadjacent AI used for vendor's internal operations only Annual review HR software, internal productivity tools

For agentic AI specifically, contracts should add explicit clauses on: acceptableuse limits for autonomous actiontaking, mandatory humanreview points, logging obligations, and — critically — a carveout acknowledgement that outsourcing the technology does not outsource the duty: the institution remains accountable to regulators and customers even when the underlying agent is entirely vendorbuilt and vendorhosted.

5.6 Governance for FintechBFSI Partnerships (BaaS and Embedded Finance)

Where a bank or NBFC partners with a fintech that embeds its own AI agents into a cobranded product (a common pattern in India's BaaS/embeddedlending ecosystem), governance must extend across the partnership boundary rather than stopping at the regulated entity's own walls. The core discipline, adapted for the Indian context, is:

  • Map what the partner's AI actually does — underwriting/pricing decisions, KYC/AML screening, or purely conversational/servicing functions — because each maps to a different governance lens and a different intensity of oversight.
  • Bring highimpact partner models into the regulated entity's own inventory and validation process, with contractual dataandmodeltesting access, bias/disparateimpact testing, and documented adverseaction reason codes for declined applicants — not reliance on the fintech's selfattestation.
  • Retain sanctions/AML/regulatory accountability with the regulated entity even where daytoday monitoring is performed by the fintech's AI, since India's AML/KYC and consumerprotection obligations sit with the licensed entity.
  • Require contractual changenotification triggers so that a silent update to the fintech's agent — a new LLM version, an adjusted riskscoring threshold — automatically triggers revalidation on the bank/NBFC side before it can affect live customers.

5.7 Culture, Incentives and the Permanent AI Standing Committee

Governance structures fail quietly when incentives point the other way — if a business head's bonus depends purely on origination volume, an agentic lending system will be pushed toward looser autonomy boundaries regardless of what the policy document says. Effective RAI governance therefore requires:

  • RAIlinked performance metrics for business owners of highrisk agentic systems (e.g., overriderate trends, fairnessmetric stability, incidentfree operation), not solely volume or cost metrics.
  • Boardlevel AI literacy, so that the RMCB can meaningfully challenge a validation report rather than rubberstamp it — a capacitybuilding priority FREEAI explicitly calls out.
  • Escalation psychology, not just escalation paths — staff must feel safe flagging an agent's anomalous behaviour without fear that doing so will be read as obstructing a revenue initiative; this is a cultural outcome that policy documents alone cannot guarantee.

At the ecosystem level, RBI's FREEAI committee itself recommended forming a permanent multistakeholder AI Standing Committee under RBI, constituted initially for five years with a builtin review mechanism, to continuously advise on emerging AI opportunities and risks and assess the ongoing relevance of the regulatory framework as agentic and generative AI capabilities evolve. BFSI institutions should treat this standing committee, once operational, as a channel for raising sectorwide agentic AI governance questions that no single institution can resolve alone — precisely the kind of forum this report's own BFSI Stakeholders AI Leaders Group is designed to feed into.

5.8 Putting It Together: A Governance Maturity Checklist

For a BFSI institution assessing its own readiness against this chapter, five questions cut to the heart of the matter:

  1. Does a boardapproved AI policy exist, with an explicit riskclassification framework and liability framework — or is AI governance still an informal, ITdepartment matter?
  2. Is there a named CAIO or equivalent, with a reporting line senior enough to block an ungoverned launch, chairing a crossfunctional steering committee?
  3. Does a RACI exist for every highrisk agentic use case, naming exactly who is accountable when an agent's action harms a customer?
  4. Is there a vendor AI inventory and tiered duediligence programme — or is the institution among the majority that cannot say which of its vendors use AI at all?
  5. Does the board actually receive and interrogate quarterly AI risk disclosures — inventory, incidents, fairness metrics — or does AI oversight exist only on paper?

An institution that can answer all five affirmatively has moved from "aspirational Responsible AI" to a genuinely governed, FREEAIaligned operating model — the organisational precondition for the sectorspecific agentic AI playbooks we turn to next.

From Governance to the Ground:

Chapters 2 through 5 built the compass, the FREEAI backbone, the lifecycle discipline and the organisational architecture. We begin with the subsector where agentic AI adoption is currently most advanced in India: Banking and NBFCs.

6.1 Banking and NBFCs

6.1.1 The Dominant Use-Case Cluster: Agentic Digital Lending — Architecture and Real-World Evidence

The single most mature agentic AI deployment pattern in Indian BFSI today is the end-to-end digital lending workflow, and it is worth examining its technical architecture in detail before turning to the regulatory overlay.

A representative production pipeline works as follows: when an MSME owner applies for working capital, an Account Aggregator (AA) integration agent pulls consented bank statements in as little as 4 seconds; a document-extraction agent built on NLP/OCR parses bureau reports, GST returns and bank statements — condensing a 900-page bureau report into actionable metrics in roughly 10 seconds; a scoring agent runs a machine-learning model (commonly gradient-boosted trees such as XGBoost or LightGBM, sometimes layered with logistic regression or decision-tree baselines for interpretability) across as many as 5,000 individual data signals spanning bureau, banking, UPI and GST sources; and a memo-compilation agent auto-generates a pre-filled credit memo with flagged anomalies and suggested personal-discussion questions, routed either straight to auto-approval or to a human credit officer. L&T Finance's Project Cyclops — its "next-generation multi-dimensional underwriting engine" — exemplifies this architecture: it supplements bureau scores with consent-based digital signals (electronically shared bank statements, payment-aggregator expense-pattern analysis, digital-footprint/phone-usage signals, and geospatial/affluence parameters), and the company has invested roughly ₹37 crore in building it. Layered above Cyclops sits Project Helios, an agentic underwriting co-pilot that has processed approximately 39,000 underwriting files, cutting SME/home-loan turnaround time from 4–5 hours to under 30 minutes, with a documented 30% TAT reduction and 1.5 hours saved per case across more than 5,000 SME files. A third layer, Project Nostradamus, fuses financial, behavioural and macroeconomic data for portfolio-level early-warning detection (live in two-wheeler finance since August 2025), with a conversational agent called Orion built on top, allowing risk teams to "chat with the data" to refine underwriting or collection parameters — illustrating how agentic layers increasingly stack on each other rather than operating as isolated point solutions. Aggregate industry evidence across NBFCs deploying similar stacks shows TAT reductions of 30–80%, approval-rate uplifts of 25–40% at stable or lower default rates, delinquency reductions of 20–50%, and operational cost reductions of 30–50%.

Technically, this is what "AI-powered credit scoring" actually does underneath the marketing language: the model ingests traditional data (repayment history, credit utilisation, loan-to-value), alternative data (bank cash-flow patterns, UPI transaction frequency, mobile-recharge regularity, rental and utility payment history), and behavioural indicators (spending-pattern shifts, digital-footprint stability, sometimes psychometric signals) — then searches for correlations that would never surface in a static, formula-based scorecard, such as a 20% dip in average monthly deposits over three consecutive months doubling default probability, or learning that seasonal income variation in certain sectors (e.g., agriculture) is a normal pattern rather than a risk signal. Critically, unlike legacy scorecards updated annually or quarterly, these models are designed to continue learning post-deployment — if real-world defaults rise among a specific borrower profile, the model adjusts — which is precisely why Chapter 4's continuous drift-monitoring discipline is not optional here but structurally necessary to the model's own design philosophy.

6.1.2 The Regulatory Overlay: RBI's Digital Lending Directions, 2025 — Implementation-Level Detail

Layered on top of FREEAI and DPDP, banking and NBFC lending agents must satisfy RBI's Digital Lending Directions, 2025, effective 8 May 2025, with app-reporting mandates phased in by June 2025 and multi-lender platform rules by November 2025. Several provisions carry precise technical implementation requirements that agentic system architects must build to, not merely acknowledge in policy:

  • Algorithmic transparency obligation. REs must document the rationale for algorithmic features aiding lending decisions; in practice, this means every feature entering the scoring model needs a documented business justification traceable back to the model card described in Chapter 4, not just an aggregate accuracy metric.
  • Granular, source-level consent capture. Consent must be obtained per data category — Account Aggregator, UPI, GST — with an audit log timestamped to each individual consent and a standing option to revoke or delete data at any time; engineering teams should implement this as per-source consent flags in the customer's data record, not a single bundled onboarding checkbox, since regulators and auditors will expect to trace exactly when and for what purpose each data source was accessed.
  • APR computation as a deterministic, regulated function. The Annual Percentage Rate must be calculated on an RBI-standardised, all-inclusive-cost methodology covering interest, processing fees, verification charges and maintenance charges (excluding only genuinely contingent charges like penal/late fees), and must be computed using actual-days-basis interest with pre-payment interest reductions applied from the actual repayment date rather than deferred to the next EMI cycle. Implementation-grade practice treats APR computation "the way payment systems treat money math: deterministic, paise-precise, with a regression test for every edge case" — a single, versioned function that both the KFS generator and the disbursement engine read from, so that no two parts of the system can ever display inconsistent rates.
  • Key Fact Statement (KFS) as a byte-exact, immutable artefact. The KFS (standardised per Annex II/F) must display APR, a full amortisation schedule broken down by principal/interest/fee, total cost of credit in rupee terms, pre-payment implications, and the cost of any ancillary bundled product (e.g., insurance); critically, any fee not disclosed in the KFS at sanction can never be charged later — meaning the fee catalogue must be a single, code-managed, versioned table that every downstream system (KFS generator, disbursement, GL posting) reads from, with the exact customer-acknowledged KFS bytes hashed and stored in immutable (WORM-style) storage for the audit trail.
  • Direct disbursal and repayment. All loan disbursals and repayments must flow directly between RE and borrower bank accounts, with no pass-through or pooling via the agent's Lending Service Provider (LSP) — architecturally, this means the agent's payment-orchestration layer must be hard-wired to bypass any LSP-held escrow account for principal movement.
  • Cooling-off period as a deterministic function. Borrowers must be able to exit the loan within a defined cooling-off window by repaying principal plus proportionate APR without penalty; implementation-grade systems treat this cooling-off calculation as "a deterministic pure function with a regression test per edge case" — zero-day exit, full-tenure exit, fee-reversal-with-stamp-duty — precisely because a miscalculated cooling-off refund is both a customer-harm event and an audit finding.
  • App registration and directory. Every Digital Lending App (DLA) — whether the RE's own or an LSP's — must be registered on RBI's Centralised Information Management System (CIMS) and appear correctly in the public DLA Directory before go-live; an agentic lending front-end that is not registered is, by definition, operating outside the regulatory perimeter.
  • No unconsented credit-limit increases, closing off a specific pattern of "helpful" autonomous upselling an agent might otherwise be tempted to build as a growth feature.
  • Universal bureau reporting. Every loan sourced through a DLA must be reported to Credit Information Companies regardless of amount or tenor — meaning an agent's decision has permanent, traceable consequences for the borrower's credit history.

6.1.3 Risk and Harm Patterns Specific to Agentic Lending

Three risk patterns recur specifically in agentic lending, distinct from the generic lifecycle risks covered in Chapter 4:

  • Silent proxy discrimination via alternative data. Because agentic underwriting fuses bureau scores with UPI, GST and geospatial/affluence signals — richer than a bureau-only model, but also richer in potential proxies for geography, informal-sector occupation, or gender-correlated spending patterns — Adverse Impact Ratio (AIR) testing (Chapter 4) becomes non-optional precisely because the feature space has structurally expanded beyond what a traditional scorecard would ever have encountered.
  • The adverse-action explainability gap. When an agent auto-declines an application, RBI's algorithmic-transparency requirement and established fair-lending practice converge: the borrower is entitled to specific, accurate reasons for denial — the actual factors the model used, generated via SHAP-based feature attribution for every credit decision — not a generic "did not meet policy criteria" catch-all.
  • LSP/agent accountability blur. Where an NBFC uses a third-party LSP's agentic platform, RBI is explicit that the RE remains accountable: the LSP's name and role must be disclosed upfront, the sanction letter must issue on the RE's own letterhead, and the RE must maintain "effective oversight and monitoring" over the platform — the same accountability-cannot-be-outsourced principle from Chapter 5, now codified into a binding sectoral rule.

6.1.4 Operational Controls: A Build-Grade Compliance Checklist

Bringing together FREEAI, the MRM draft and the Digital Lending Directions — and drawing on documented industry implementation practice — a responsibly deployed agentic lending workflow should embed:

  • At intake: per-source consent flags (AA/UPI/GST/bureau) captured as discrete, timestamped, auditable records — never a single bundled checkbox.
  • At scoring: AIR/SMD fairness testing re-run on the full alternative-data feature set every time a new data source (e.g., GST Phase 2, geospatial layer) is added — not only at initial model launch — since each new signal reopens the proxy-discrimination question.
  • At decisioning: SHAP-based feature attribution generating plain-language, borrower-facing reason codes automatically as part of the agent's output, not reconstructed on customer request.
  • At disbursal: a hard architectural gate preventing any disbursal or repayment from flowing through an LSP pass-through account — enforced at the payment-rails level, not as a policy instruction to the agent.
  • At the customer interface: KFS presentation and digital acknowledgment as non-bypassable workflow steps, with the exact acknowledged bytes hashed into immutable, 10-year-retention storage, and a visible, correctly-computed cooling-off window.
  • At the platform level: every DLA registered on RBI's CIMS portal and appearing correctly in the public DLA Directory before go-live, with Chief Compliance Officer sign-off — a named, non-negotiable item in the Chapter 5 deployment gate.
  • On an ongoing basis: universal CIC bureau reporting for every agent-originated loan, quarterly drift monitoring following a mandatory parallel-run period (commonly 6 weeks alongside manual underwriting before full cutover), and continuous tracking of human-underwriter override rates by applicant segment as the earliest signal of model drift or emergent bias — exactly as illustrated by the collections-agent example in Chapter 4.

Collections and AML/KYC agentic workflows within banking/NBFCs follow the same underlying discipline — already illustrated in depth through the collections-agent worked example in Chapter 4 — and should be read as sibling use cases within this same sector playbook rather than requiring separate first-principles treatment.

6.2 Securities and Capital Markets

6.2.1 Context: A Sector Where AI Liability Has Already Been Codified

Securities and capital markets are unique among BFSI sub-sectors in one important respect: SEBI has moved beyond principles and reporting into binding, liability-fixing regulation specifically for AI/ML, and has done so with unusually granular technical prescriptions rather than high-level policy language. Three instruments now layer together — the 2019 AI/ML reporting circulars, Regulation 16C (inserted via the SEBI (Intermediaries) (Amendment) Regulations, 2025, effective 10 February 2025), and the retail algorithmic-trading framework of February 2025, made fully operative from 1 April 2026 — creating one of the most codified AI accountability regimes in Indian financial regulation. For agentic AI, this sub-sector is instructive precisely because it shows what "accountability cannot be outsourced" (Chapter 5) looks like once a regulator writes it into a standalone regulation with exact API-level specifications, rather than leaving it as sector-wide expectation.

6.2.2 Dominant Agentic AI Use-Case Clusters

Agentic AI is emerging across capital markets in three distinct clusters, each with a different risk profile:

  • Algorithmic execution agents — software that autonomously decides trade timing, sizing and routing based on market signals, now extended to retail investors through broker APIs rather than confined to institutional desks.
  • Robo-advisory and research agents — AI tools that generate investment advice, portfolio recommendations or research reports, deployed by Investment Advisers (IAs) and Research Analysts (RAs).
  • Surveillance and compliance agents — AI used by brokers, depositories and market infrastructure institutions (MIIs) for trade surveillance, KYC, and abnormal-pattern detection — a defensive rather than trading-facing use case, but one still squarely inside the reporting regime.

6.2.3 The Regulatory Overlay: Three Instruments Working Together, With Implementation-Grade Detail

Instrument 1 — AI/ML Reporting Circulars (2019, harmonised 2025). Registered stock brokers, depository participants, MIIs and mutual funds must file structured AI/ML disclosures — application name and type, first-use date, whether the tool influences order execution, whether it enables discretionary investment or portfolio management, cybersecurity use, marketing claims made about the tool, abnormal-behaviour safeguards, and a compliance attestation. Trading-member reporting occurs half-yearly through exchanges' Enhanced Supervision Portal, and since July 2025, NSE-registered members file a single harmonised disclosure that NSE shares across BSE, MSE, MCX and NCDEX — reducing duplicate filing but increasing the completeness expected in a single submission.

Instrument 2 — Regulation 16C: Sole Liability for AI/ML Outputs. This is the sharpest edge of SEBI's framework. Regulation 16C establishes that any SEBI-regulated entity is solely liable for AI/ML tools it uses — whether developed in-house or procured externally — covering data privacy and security, integrity of AI outputs, and compliance with all applicable laws; non-compliance triggers SEBI Act 1992 enforcement, including monetary sanctions, cease-and-desist orders and suspension. Parallel amendments extend an equivalent standard to MIIs and depositories. For agentic AI, Regulation 16C removes any ambiguity Chapter 5 raised about vendor accountability: there is no "the vendor's model made the error" defence available to a SEBI-regulated entity — the liability sits entirely with the regulated firm, regardless of who built or trained the underlying agent.

Instrument 3 — IA/RA AI Guidance. SEBI's October 2024 consultation on IA/RA regulations addresses AI use directly: responsibility for investment advisory or research services lies solely with the IA/RA, irrespective of the scale or scenario of AI tool use, and any IA/RA using an AI tool to service clients "must provide complete disclosure of the extent of use of such tools to its prospective clients" to enable an informed decision on whether to continue the relationship. Using an AI tool does not dilute the underlying suitability obligation — SEBI is explicit that "requirements of risk profiling and suitability have been complied with" remains the IA's responsibility regardless of AI involvement.

Instrument 4 — Safer Participation of Retail Investors in Algorithmic Trading (February 2025, effective 1 April 2026). This circular restructures how algorithmic/agentic trading reaches retail clients, with technical specifications precise enough to constitute an actual implementation blueprint:

  • Broker-as-principal, algo provider-as-agent. Every algo provider must route through a broker rather than dealing directly with the exchange; brokers deal with empanelled algo providers only and handle all related complaints, since such providers are legally the broker's agents.
  • Universal Algo ID and Client → Algo-ID → Static IP → API Key traceability chain. Every algorithmic order — client-generated or vendor-supplied — must carry a unique exchange-assigned identifier, and every order must be traceable through the full chain: Client → Algo-ID → Static IP → API Key/user, enabling exact audit and compliance tracing of any single order back to its originating agent and infrastructure.
  • Order-Per-Second (OPS) threshold: exactly 10 OPS. Orders below 10 per second per exchange per client require no formal registration; above the threshold, the algorithm must be registered with each exchange where it trades — a hard, machine-enforceable rate limit rather than a qualitative "high-frequency" judgment call.
  • Static IP architecture — the precise technical rules. A client may register one static IP (primary) or add a second static IP (secondary) for connectivity redundancy; multiple API keys can map to the same or different primary/secondary IPs; a static IP can be mapped to only one client at a time, though it may be shared within a defined "family" (self, spouse, dependent children/parents) via a written, registered-email, or 2FA-validated request; and clients may update their mapped static IP no more than once per calendar week, except in extraordinary circumstances requiring broker escalation. In case of client-generated algos, the static IP must be the client's own; for algos generated via empanelled providers using a Vendor API Key, the static IP is the vendor's or the client's; for broker-generated algos, it is the broker's or the client's.
  • OAuth-only authentication, mandatory 2FA, and daily session expiry. Brokers must use OAuth (Open Authentication)-based authentication exclusively — all other authentication mechanisms must be discontinued — and must authenticate every API session through two-factor authentication; API sessions automatically close at the end of each trading day, requiring fresh 2FA the next morning. Developer-level implementation guidance recommends TOTP (Time-based One-Time Password via an authenticator app) as the primary second factor for daily active users, with SMS OTP as a fallback channel, and mandates that static-IP verification occur before the 2FA challenge is even triggered — rejecting requests from unwhitelisted IPs at the firewall/middleware layer to prevent credential-probing and unnecessary SMS costs.
  • White Box vs. Black Box classification — with a critical technical nuance. "Execution Algos" (White Box) are transparent and replicable — TWAP (Time-Weighted Average Price) and VWAP (Volume-Weighted Average Price) strategies are the explicitly named examples of algos that remain in the lighter-touch category. Crucially, any strategy other than TWAP/VWAP is deemed a Black Box Algo by default — even if its logic is fully disclosed and open — meaning a novel, transparent, rules-based agentic strategy can still trigger Black Box obligations purely because it isn't TWAP/VWAP. For Black Box Algos, the provider must register as a Research Analyst, maintain a detailed research report per algo, submit a written risk-management-system (RMS) description, provide an auditor certificate, and undertake to maintain a full trade-level report reproducible to the exchange/regulator on demand; no modification is permitted to a registered Black Box algo — any change to the underlying logic requires fresh registration as an entirely new algo with a new research report, and algos must be hosted on the Trading Member's own server (a provider cannot host Black Box algos for multiple third-party RAs on shared infrastructure). This has a direct, sharp implication for agentic AI: an LLM-driven or reinforcement-learning-based trading agent — whose decision logic is neither TWAP/VWAP nor fully replicable — falls automatically into this heaviest-obligation category, with every model update legally equivalent to launching a brand-new algorithm.
  • Security stack, per the exchange's technical circular. Mandatory features include user ID and first-level password with automatic expiry and re-initialisation after a reasonable duration, complete transaction logs with audit facilities, secure end-to-end encryption between client and broker server, mutual authentication procedures, and IP-address capture for every order — all layered beneath the OAuth/2FA/static-IP stack described above.
  • Exchange-level kill switches. Exchanges retain explicit authority to deploy kill switches on erratic or non-compliant algorithmic strategies — an exchange-level backstop control sitting above the institution's own internal kill-switch mechanisms discussed in Chapter 4.
  • Mandatory pre-trade RMS checks. Price, quantity and value limits, margin sufficiency, position and exposure limits, and self-trade/loop prevention, enforced at the broker level before any algo order reaches the market.

6.2.4 Risk and Harm Patterns Specific to Agentic Capital-Markets Use

Three risk patterns are distinctive to this sub-sector:

  • Explainability as a registration trigger, not just a governance nice-to-have. In banking (Section 6.1), explainability primarily serves adverse-action disclosure to a borrower. In capital markets, explainability determines the regulatory category itself: because only TWAP/VWAP strategies escape Black Box classification by default, a genuinely novel agentic execution strategy is functionally guaranteed to trigger RA registration, a research report, an RMS write-up and an auditor certificate — meaning explainability engineering (Chapter 4) is not optional risk mitigation but a determinant of which regulatory pathway, and which fixed-cost compliance burden, an institution must follow.
  • Mis-selling and suitability erosion through robo-advisory agents. An agentic advisory tool that generates portfolio recommendations at scale risks subtly drifting from a documented risk-profiling methodology, especially if the underlying model is periodically retrained on new market data — SEBI's guidance closes off any argument that "the AI decided this, not us" by fixing suitability responsibility on the IA/RA regardless of AI involvement.
  • Speed-risk and systemic contagion, engineered against via hard machine limits. Because algorithmic and agentic execution operates at machine speed, a flawed or drifting agent can generate market impact — self-reinforcing order loops, erroneous mass order placement — far faster than a human-paced error, which is precisely why SEBI layers pre-trade RMS checks, the 10 OPS registration threshold, and exchange-level kill switches as hard, real-time technical controls (not merely policy statements) that will mechanically halt or flag a runaway agent regardless of what its own internal logic intended.

6.2.5 Operational Controls: A Build-Grade Compliance Checklist

Bringing FREEAI, DPDP, the MRM lifecycle discipline (Chapter 4) and SEBI's regime together, a responsibly deployed agentic trading or advisory system should embed:

  • At registration: empanel with each relevant exchange as an algo provider (or confirm the broker has done so); classify the agent's decision logic honestly — presume Black Box unless the strategy is genuinely TWAP/VWAP — and, if Black Box, register the provider as a Research Analyst with a per-algo research report, RMS write-up and auditor certificate before go-live.
  • At the API/infrastructure layer: implement OAuth-only authentication, mandatory 2FA (TOTP-primary, SMS fallback) validated after static-IP whitelist checks, one primary and optionally one secondary static IP per client (updatable at most weekly), unique vendor/client-specific API keys, and automatic daily session expiry — architected as a single, auditable Client → Algo-ID → Static IP → API Key traceability chain for every order.
  • At the reporting layer: ensure half-yearly AI/ML disclosure filings (harmonised across NSE/BSE/MSE/MCX/NCDEX since July 2025) capture the agent accurately — application type, first-use date, order-execution involvement, discretionary-investment use, and abnormal-behaviour safeguards — treated as a live document updated whenever the agent's function materially changes.
  • At the execution layer: enforce the 10 OPS registration threshold, unique Algo ID tagging on every order, and pre-trade RMS checks (price/quantity/value limits, margin sufficiency, self-trade prevention) as non-bypassable, broker-enforced gates ahead of the agent's order reaching the exchange.
  • At the change-management layer: treat any modification to a registered Black Box algo's logic as a mandatory fresh registration with a new research report — never a routine model update — and host all Black Box algo infrastructure exclusively on the Trading Member's own server, never on shared multi-RA vendor infrastructure.
  • At the advisory layer: provide explicit, client-facing disclosure of the extent of AI/agentic tool use in generating advice or research, and maintain documented evidence that risk-profiling and suitability assessments were genuinely completed — not inferred by the AI tool — for every client interaction the agent touches.
  • At the governance layer: because Regulation 16C fixes sole liability on the regulated entity, the Chapter 5 vendor-governance programme becomes non-negotiable here — every third-party algo provider or AI vendor contract must include audit rights, model-change notification, and a clear acknowledgment that liability for AI-driven outputs remains with the regulated entity regardless of contractual risk allocation with the vendor.
  • At the incident layer: maintain readiness for exchange-level kill-switch intervention — meaning internal kill-switch and circuit-breaker mechanisms (Chapter 4) should be designed to interoperate with, not conflict against, an exchange's own emergency shutdown authority over an erratic algorithm.

6.3 Insurance (Revised — Technical Deep-Dive)

6.3.1 Context: A Sector Moving From Conduct Norms to a Dedicated AI Framework

Insurance is the BFSI sub-sector where the regulatory picture is currently most in flux, and it demands careful sequencing. Until mid-2026, IRDAI had no AI-specific rulebook; agentic AI in underwriting, pricing and claims sat entirely under existing conduct-and-policyholder-protection norms. That changed on 19 June 2026, when IRDAI constituted a 7-member AI Working Group — chaired by Prof. Sandeep K. Shukla (IIIT Hyderabad), with members from CERT-In, ReBIT, and life/general/health insurers — with a three-month mandate to deliver India's first formal insurance-sector AI governance framework, explicitly naming claims processing and fraud detection as the highest-risk use cases, and tasked with designing India's first pre- and post-deployment AI audit framework. This followed IRDAI's April 2026 cybersecurity guidelines, which already required insurers to report on AI cyber-readiness by 22 May 2026 — showing the regulator moving from soft guidance to hard obligation in rapid succession.

Until this Working Group's output is finalised and notified, the operative legal anchors for agentic AI in Indian insurance remain the IRDAI Master Circular on Protection of Policyholders' Interests, 2024 (issued 5 September 2024, superseding 30 prior circulars) and general conduct, suitability and outsourcing norms. BFSI institutions should treat this section as a dual-track playbook: apply today's binding policyholder-protection obligations now, while pre-positioning for the AI Working Group's framework.

6.3.2 Dominant Agentic AI Use-Case Clusters — With Production Architecture Detail

Cluster 1 — Motor claims photogrammetry. A representative production pipeline, documented in a live Bajaj Allianz deployment, runs in four stages. Stage 1 (Image Capture Protocol): the customer is prompted via app/WhatsApp to photograph the vehicle from a minimum of four defined exterior angles plus targeted damage-area shots — full 360-degree coverage rather than isolated damage images, since downstream model accuracy depends directly on capture completeness. Stage 2 (Image Validation and Quality Scoring): a pre-processing layer checks resolution, blur, lighting adequacy and angle-coverage completeness, rejecting and prompting re-capture for images that fail quality thresholds — a critical control, since low-quality images silently degrade downstream assessment reliability in ways that are hard to detect post-hoc. Stage 3 (Damage Classification and Estimation): the validated image set enters a hierarchical classification model — first determining whether damage is surface-level (paint, trim, minor dents, routed to rapid settlement) or structural (panel deformation, frame damage, mechanical exposure, routed to an enhanced human review queue) — with the model trained on historical claims images labelled by experienced surveyors, so it learns surveyor-equivalent classification logic rather than generic computer-vision patterns. Under the hood, this typically runs two parallel deep-learning models: a Car Parts Detection model that segments granular vehicle components, and a Damage Detection model that identifies and classifies damage type — feeding a combined output of affected-component list, repair-vs-replacement recommendation, and settlement-estimate range. Stage 4 (Workflow Orchestration): the classification output feeds an agent-orchestration layer that routes the claim to instant settlement (low-value, low-risk, surface-level), enhanced review (structural or ambiguous images), or fraud-investigation escalation (anomalous metadata or damage inconsistencies) — with a full end-to-end cycle from photograph to settlement offer completed in roughly 20 minutes, with no surveyor dispatched for eligible claims.

Cluster 2 — Multi-agent fraud detection at First Notice of Loss (FNOL). Modern architecture deploys multiple specialised agents in parallel the moment a claim is filed, rather than a single post-hoc fraud model: a Voice Agent handles intake conversation, listening for inconsistency signals (timeline contradictions, hesitation patterns, unusual familiarity with claim procedure); a Document Agent runs photogrammetric analysis on damage photographs, cross-references repair-cost estimates against historical data for the same vehicle class, and flags metadata anomalies indicating prior image edits; and a Data Agent queries unified customer history for policy-timeline red flags and prior claim patterns — with the combined tiered fraud-risk score (not a binary flag) surfaced to the claims handler within minutes of intake, before any surveyor is dispatched and before payment is initiated. Production-grade fraud-scoring architectures run three parallel models per claim: a gradient-boosting classifier scoring tabular risk features, a graph neural network scoring entity-relationship risk, and an anomaly-detection model scoring behavioural deviation from historical baselines — combined by a meta-model into a single risk rank with itemised evidence, delivered to the investigator workflow in under 500 milliseconds. The underlying feature set spans four categories: entity features (claimant/provider/vehicle historical behaviour), velocity features (claim frequency, severity trends, timing patterns), network features (connectivity to known fraud-associated entities), and contextual features (deviation from expected patterns given claim type, geography, season).

Cluster 3 — Graph-based fraud-ring detection. For organised, collusive fraud (garage-surveyor-agent nexuses, staged accidents, phantom hospitalisations), Indian insurers increasingly build a graph where nodes represent policyholders, vehicles, drivers, garages, hospitals, doctors, surveyors, loss assessors, agents, brokers, addresses, phone numbers, bank accounts, and IFSC-bank-branch combinations, with edges capturing policy ownership, claim filing, treatment provision, professional involvement and shared identifiers. Production graphs for major Indian insurers typically contain tens of millions of nodes and hundreds of millions of edges built across multi-year claims history, stored in dedicated graph-database technology (Neo4j, JanusGraph, TigerGraph, or insurer-internal graph stores), with graph neural networks learning entity embeddings that capture both individual features and network position; annual infrastructure costs for this layer alone range from ₹1.5 to ₹8 crore for the largest insurers running multi-product fraud detection. Operationally, fraud detection runs in parallel with, not blocking, standard claims processing — claims continue through normal channels while flagged claims enter a prioritised investigation queue, with payment held pending investigation outcome.

Cluster 4 — Underwriting assistant agents. Agents that prioritise high-risk/high-value submissions, summarise documents, extract underwriting data, and predict claim probability and pricing sensitivity ahead of first-notice-of-loss, feeding rating engines directly.

6.3.3 The Regulatory Overlay: Policyholder Protection Today, AI Governance Tomorrow

Current binding obligations (Master Circular, 2024). The Master Circular consolidates policyholder entitlements and imposes several obligations directly relevant to agentic claims and underwriting systems: mandatory turnaround times (TATs) for policy processing and claims settlement, meaning an agentic workflow's speed advantage is a compliance lever provided speed doesn't compromise accuracy or fairness; documented suitability assessment for savings-related life and annuity products, a duty that cannot be discharged merely by pointing to an AI recommendation; a Citizens' Charter and grievance infrastructure (Integrated Grievance Management System, Bima Bharosa) that must remain the customer's route to challenge any AI-influenced decision; and a Free Look Period (30 days for life/health) that must be preserved intact even where onboarding is agent-driven end-to-end.

The emerging AI-specific framework. Drawing on IRDAI's stated Working Group mandate, insurers should expect the eventual framework to require, at minimum: (1) explainability for adverse AI decisions — claim denials and premium loadings accompanied by a plain-language statement of principal driving factors; (2) human-in-the-loop for high-stakes claims — no high-impact decision finalised solely by an automated system, with de novo human escalation; (3) prohibition of proxy discrimination; and (4) pre- and post-deployment audit obligations, including sandbox-style testing before wide release and periodic bias/drift re-testing thereafter. A detailed draft policy framework circulating in Indian insurance-policy discourse — the proposed "IRDAI (Algorithmic Accountability and Transparency) Guidelines" — illustrates the likely shape: mandatory Human-in-the-Loop review for any "High-Impact Decision" (claim denial, coverage rejection, or premium increase above 15%), a prohibition on opaque "price optimisation" algorithms pricing on willingness-to-pay rather than actuarial risk, a mandatory "Key Factor Statement" listing top variables behind a negative decision, an internal Algorithmic Ethics Committee certifying algorithms as bias-free before deployment, and a phased enforcement timeline (disclosure → third-party audit → full enforcement) with penalties up to 2% of Gross Written Premium for Human-in-the-Loop violations.

6.3.4 Risk and Harm Patterns Specific to Agentic Insurance Use — Technically Grounded

  • Millisecond denial without reasoning capture. Health-claims agents can validate and deny coverage in milliseconds, per the Layer-1 ML fraud-scoring architecture described above; without a captured reasoning trace tied to the specific features that drove the score (provider billing percentile, patient utilisation pattern, diagnostic-code plausibility, billing-timing clustering), an insurer cannot answer a policyholder's dispute or a grievance officer's de novo review.
  • Price-optimisation drift into unfair discrimination, and proxy discrimination via correlated variables (surname, pin code, regional language substituting for caste, religion or class) — both risks compounded in a graph-based architecture, since network-proximity features (shared garage, shared surveyor, shared address) can inadvertently encode socioeconomic or community clustering that correlates with protected characteristics rather than genuine fraud risk.
  • Fraud false positives harming genuine claimants, particularly acute in graph-based detection: a legitimate claimant who happens to share a garage, hospital or surveyor with a fraud ring can be flagged purely by network proximity (high degree/eigenvector centrality or Louvain community membership) even with zero individual wrongdoing — precisely why fairness testing and human review must cover flagged-but-innocent claims, not just approved ones.
  • Image-quality-driven assessment errors. Because photogrammetry accuracy is directly dependent on input image quality, and structural damage explicitly requires surveyor referral rather than automated settlement, any weakening of the Stage 2 validation gate (accepting borderline images to speed throughput) directly degrades assessment reliability in ways that are difficult to detect after the fact.

6.3.5 Operational Controls: Building an IRDAI-Ready Agentic Insurance Stack

Bringing together the current Master Circular obligations, the anticipated AI Working Group framework, and the production architectures described above, insurers should implement five concrete gates before any claims, underwriting or fraud agent goes live:

  1. Agent registration with autonomy classification. Every agent (Voice, Document, Data, Fraud-Scoring, Photogrammetry) registered in an enterprise registry with a unique identity, version, owner and explicit risk classification; autonomy level defined as fully autonomous, supervised, or human-in-the-loop; permitted actions, data access and settlement-value ceilings documented — no unregistered agents in production.
  2. Mandatory reasoning capture for every decision, with the gradient-boosting, graph-neural-network and anomaly-detection component scores retained individually — not just the meta-model's combined output — so a disputed decision can be decomposed back to its specific contributing signal.
  3. Human-in-the-loop gates calibrated to the damage/claim classification itself: surface-level, low-value, low-fraud-risk claims may auto-settle; structural damage, high-value claims, or any claim above a defined fraud-risk-score threshold route automatically to a human reviewer for genuine de novo review, mirroring the Bajaj Allianz routing logic.
  4. Continuous fairness and network-bias monitoring, explicitly testing whether graph-centrality-driven fraud flags disproportionately affect specific geographies, communities or socioeconomic segments — not just testing the tabular-feature model in isolation.
  5. Key Factor Statements and grievance integration, with every adverse decision generating a plain-language statement of top contributing factors (drawn from the retained component scores in control #2), delivered alongside the decision and wired directly into the Integrated Grievance Management System.

At the governance layer, insurers should also apply the Chapter 5 vendor-governance discipline with particular rigour here: because multi-agent fraud, photogrammetry and graph-analytics systems are frequently sourced from specialised insurtech vendors (AgentVerse/DataVerse-style platforms, dedicated graph-database vendors), contracts must include continuing due-diligence obligations covering training-data provenance, known failure modes on Indian-specific fraud patterns, and periodic re-testing, since the conduct duty to the policyholder cannot be discharged by pointing to a vendor's black box.

6.4 Payments, Fintech and New-Age Players

6.4.1 Context: The Highest-Velocity, Highest-Volume Agentic AI Battleground

Payments is where agentic AI operates at a speed and scale unlike any other BFSI sub-sector: PhonePe alone processes over 31 crore (310 million) daily transactions, with fraud-decisioning models required to respond in single-digit to low double-digit milliseconds inside the payment authorization path itself. This sub-sector spans three overlapping populations — banks and PSOs operating payment rails, payment aggregators and fintech intermediaries, and "new-age" super-app/BNPL/neo-bank players — each carrying a distinct regulatory perimeter even where the underlying agentic AI stack looks architecturally similar. This section examines all three, anchored in real, publicly documented production systems rather than generic descriptions.

6.4.2 Payments Infrastructure: Technical Architecture of India's National Fraud-Intelligence Layer

RBI's MuleHunter.AI, live across 26+ banks, is a rule-and-ML hybrid engine that ingests transaction patterns, device fingerprints and behavioural anomalies to identify mule-account networks used for laundering fraudulent proceeds — the first national-scale deployment of AI directly by the central bank rather than left entirely to individual institutions.

IDPIC (Indian Digital Payment Intelligence Corporation) — incorporated 16 October 2025 as a Section 8 not-for-profit under the Companies Act 2013, with SBI investing ₹100 crore for a 50% stake and Bank of Baroda as co-promoter, authorised capital ₹500 crore, paid-up ₹20 crore initially — is the technical embodiment of RBI's Digital Payments Intelligence Platform (DPIP), built by the Reserve Bank Innovation Hub (RBIH) and operated by IDPIC as a neutral utility. Its architecture is worth understanding precisely because it defines the API contract every bank, NBFC, payment aggregator, PPI and fintech will need to integrate against:

  • Five core API services: Ingestion (a bank reports a suspected customer and their identifiers into the central registry — the "Smart Registry"); Screening (any participating institution checks an identifier against the registry in real time and receives a match status plus risk score); Feedback (institutions report back what action they took after a match, which feeds model improvement); Revoke (withdraw an earlier report after re-examination clears the customer); and Dispute (challenge another bank's entry, routed back to the reporting institution for review).
  • Privacy-by-design hashing: identifiers are hashed inside the reporting bank before transmission, so the central registry never holds readable phone numbers or account numbers — only fixed-length hashed strings — with all participants using the same hashing scheme so matching still works without any institution seeing another's raw customer data. This is a concrete, replicable pattern for DPDP-compliant, privacy-preserving cross-institutional AI collaboration that any BFSI institution's own data-sharing architecture should study.
  • Self-learning model: trained on 13 months of anonymised transaction history plus connected-account graph analysis, with real-time transaction risk scores delivered via a standardised Risk Scoring API to banks, networks, payment aggregators and PPIs before a payment is authorised — explicitly supporting an Allow / Flag / Block decision at the point of transaction.
  • Current phase limitation, stated candidly by its own documentation: DPIP today shares only reported intelligence — what banks have already seen, checked and confirmed — and does not yet independently predict fraud using its own ML models; predictive fraud-scoring is explicitly slated as "Phase 2." This is an important nuance for institutions building their own agentic fraud systems: IDPIC currently augments, but does not replace, an institution's own real-time ML fraud engine.

NPCI's UPI-level technical implementation embeds risk scoring directly into the transaction message schema itself: every UPI payment request and response XML carries a <RiskScores> element with <Score provider="sp" type="TXNRISK" value=""/> (the sponsor bank/PSP's own risk score) and <Score provider="npci" type="TXNRISK" value=""/> (NPCI's own centrally computed score) — meaning risk assessment is not a separate side-channel API call but a first-class field in the payment protocol itself, evaluated on every single transaction. NPCI additionally imposes hard technical guardrails agentic systems must respect: a maximum of three transaction-status polling attempts with a minimum 90-second interval between each, and tiered auto-reversal timeout thresholds (0–30 seconds for network/technical failures, 30 seconds–5 minutes for normal processing delays with status polling, 5–15 minutes as the maximum wait before automatic fund credit-back) — an agent that polls more aggressively than this, or fails to implement idempotency keys to prevent duplicate debits on retry, would itself become a source of customer harm and API throttling.

6.4.3 Fintech: PhonePe's Guardrails — A Detailed, Publicly Documented Production Case

PhonePe's own engineering blog provides one of the most granular public accounts of production-grade agentic fraud AI in Indian BFSI, making it worth examining as a worked technical example.

The Guardrails engine runs a four-layer architecture evaluated on every transaction: (1) Connected Intelligence — maps relationships between users, devices and merchants using graph techniques to detect coordinated fraud rings, structurally identical to the insurance graph-fraud architecture in Section 6.3; (2) Action Intelligence — monitors behaviour patterns and usage frequency to catch anomalies before they escalate; (3) Profile Intelligence — dynamically scores sender, receiver and payment instrument in real time; and (4) Behavioural Biometrics — flags subtle deviations such as typing rhythm, device grip and location shifts that reveal account takeover, running via an Edge Framework that executes ML models directly on the user's device rather than in the cloud, eliminating network latency and avoiding sending raw behavioural data off-device — a privacy-preserving architectural choice directly responsive to DPDP data-minimisation principles.

The underlying P2P fraud model, documented in technical detail, was built by first segmenting historical fraud transactions using demographic, behavioural and historical transaction-pattern signals, then training and tuning multiple classification algorithms to score sender, receiver and transaction jointly. The team explicitly chose a real-time scoring model over a daily batch model, despite the added engineering complexity, because batch scoring would have let fraud complete before detection — with two hard technical challenges solved specifically for this: maintaining real-time feature aggregates (solved via an internal low-latency knowledge store called "Yoda," feeding the ML flow servers) and ensuring the real-time evaluation engine ("Kratos") returns a decision within the payment flow's permitted time budget, achieved partly by pre-filtering transactions using insights from the earlier segmentation exercise to reduce load on the live-scoring path. Documented outcomes: a 40% reduction in consumer-to-consumer fraud rate, a 38% overall reduction in daily fraud complaints (50% in the specifically targeted high-value segment), and a blacklisting reinstatement rate of roughly 1% — indicating the model's block decisions were rarely successfully appealed as false positives, a useful proxy fairness/accuracy signal.

PhonePe's "Financial Risk Indicator (FRI)" integration illustrates cross-institutional agentic collaboration in practice: in May 2025, India's Department of Telecommunications launched FRI, an AI-powered fraud-detection network flagging suspicious activity across the payments ecosystem using telecom-side signals (SIM-swap patterns, device/IMEI anomalies); PhonePe was the first integrator, and the partnership blocked 48 lakh (4.8 million) suspicious transactions, prevented an estimated ₹125 crore in fraud, cut fraud complaints by 40%, and achieved a 1% false-positive rate at national scale — demonstrating that telecom-sector and payments-sector agentic systems can be fused into a single fraud-intelligence pipeline when data-sharing architecture (privacy-preserving hashing, as with IDPIC) is designed correctly.

PhonePe's internal "Agent Hub" platform — built specifically because commercial AI tools created integration friction — offers a rare public glimpse into engineering-grade agentic infrastructure controls: production agents run in ephemeral Docker containers with production-grade isolation (no network access, strict process-ID limits), orchestrated with CPU pinning for precise resource allocation; sensitive internal MCP (Model Context Protocol) servers run air-gapped, spawned as local subprocesses with zero public-internet dependency to satisfy strict data-privacy requirements; and the platform deliberately mixes open-source, self-hosted models for routine, cost-sensitive tasks with paid frontier models reserved only for complex-reasoning tasks. In fraud and risk operations specifically, PhonePe reports AI now drives 80% of end-to-end fraud investigations, with complex multi-modal investigations that previously took 90 minutes now completed instantly, cost per investigation falling from ₹50 to between ₹5–15, and the system autonomously handling roughly 800 cases per period that previously had no human bandwidth to cover at all. This is a concrete, quantified illustration of the "augment, don't just automate, capacity" argument running through this report — agentic AI here expanded investigative coverage, not merely investigative speed.

6.4.4 New-Age Players: BNPL, Neo-Banks and Super-Apps — Where Lending Rules Meet Payments Speed

Where fintechs and super-apps layer digital lending onto a payments experience, the full weight of the Digital Lending Directions, 2025 discussed in Section 6.1 applies without dilution — KFS disclosure, cooling-off periods, direct disbursal, DLA registration on RBI's CIMS — regardless of how seamlessly the lending decision is embedded inside a checkout flow. The technical challenge unique to this population is that BNPL underwriting must resolve in the same sub-second-to-few-second window as a payment authorisation, compressing the Section 6.1 credit-decisioning pipeline (AA pull, bureau check, alternative-data scoring, KFS generation) into a checkout-speed budget that leaves far less room for the human-in-the-loop escalation pattern used in standard digital lending — meaning fairness testing, explainability and KFS-generation logic must all be validated and correct before launch, since a real-time human-review fallback is often commercially unworkable at checkout speed.

RBI's own 2021 Working Group on Digital Lending anticipated exactly this tension, recommending that algorithms used for underwriting be auditable and "knitted in ethical AI design," with algorithm audits specifically checking for potential discrimination factors in credit availability and pricing, and encouraging DLAs to favour "glass-box" models over opaque ones wherever feasible — a direct echo of the White Box/Black Box distinction SEBI applies to trading algorithms in Section 6.2, now applied to BNPL credit-scoring logic.

The Payment Aggregator Master Direction (PA-MD, September 2025) adds a further architectural constraint for super-apps and neo-banks combining payments and lending: it tightens fund-flow rules, imposes stronger governance and net-worth requirements, restricts split-settlement models to large merchants, and mandates full KYC for all large merchants — meaning any agentic payment-routing or settlement-orchestration system embedded in a super-app must be re-architected around merchant relationships that are contractually and KYC-verified up front, not dynamically discovered at transaction time.

6.4.5 Risk and Harm Patterns Specific to Agentic Payments and Fintech

  • The fraud-versus-friction trade-off, engineered wrong in either direction. PhonePe's own case shows the correct calibration: a 40% fraud reduction alongside a low 1% reinstatement rate (few wrongly blocked users) demonstrates that real-time ML scoring can reduce fraud without proportionally increasing false positives — but this required substantial dedicated infrastructure (Yoda, Kratos) precisely to avoid the naive failure mode of an agent tuned purely for fraud-loss minimisation blocking too many legitimate transactions.
  • Cross-institutional data-sharing risk and shared-model accountability. As fraud detection moves toward federated, cross-bank risk-scoring (MuleHunter.AI, IDPIC's Screening API, NPCI's embedded <RiskScores> schema), institutions face a governance question Chapters 4–5 did not need to address in single-institution contexts: whose model-risk-management framework governs a shared, multi-party AI system, and who is accountable if the shared score — not the institution's own model — is wrong or stale (IDPIC's current Phase-1 limitation of sharing only reported, not independently predicted, intelligence is a direct illustration of this boundary).
  • Polling and retry logic as an underappreciated technical-compliance risk. NPCI's explicit three-attempt/90-second polling limits and tiered reversal timeouts show that agentic payment-status-checking logic itself is now a regulated technical surface — an agent that retries too aggressively risks API throttling and NPCI non-compliance, independent of whether its underlying fraud-scoring is accurate.
  • BNPL underwriting compressed into checkout-speed decisions, leaving materially less room for human escalation than standard digital lending, raising the stakes on getting fairness testing and explainability right before launch rather than relying on a real-time fallback.
  • Liability for agentic hallucination in customer commitments. Unlike a passive chatbot, an agent authorised to take payment, refund or credit actions is legally exposed if it "hallucinates" a false promise or an unauthorised transaction — the institution is bound by the commitment the agent made, not merely embarrassed by an incorrect answer.

6.4.6 Operational Controls: Building a Responsible Agentic Payments/Fintech Stack

  1. Layered, multi-signal fraud scoring rather than single-model reliance, modelled on PhonePe's four-layer Guardrails pattern (connected/network, behavioural-action, profile, biometric) rather than a single fraud-probability model.
  2. Edge/on-device inference for sensitive behavioural signals wherever feasible, to minimise raw data transmission and align with DPDP data-minimisation principles, following PhonePe's Edge Framework precedent.
  3. Dedicated low-latency feature stores and evaluation engines (a "Yoda"/"Kratos"-equivalent pattern) built specifically to keep real-time scoring within the payment-authorisation time budget, rather than retrofitting a batch-trained model into a real-time path and hoping latency holds.
  4. Interoperability with national fraud infrastructure: integrate with IDPIC's Ingestion/Screening/Feedback API cycle and NPCI's embedded <RiskScores> schema, applying the same hashing-before-transmission discipline IDPIC itself uses, so participation in shared intelligence never requires exposing raw customer identifiers.
  5. Respect protocol-level rate limits as release-blocking requirements: implement NPCI's three-attempt/90-second polling ceiling, tiered auto-reversal timeouts, and idempotency keys on every payment request to prevent duplicate debits on retry — treated as hard technical gates, not best-effort guidelines.
  6. Production-grade agent sandboxing: run agentic workflows in isolated, network-restricted execution environments with strict resource limits (per PhonePe's Agent Hub pattern), and keep any air-gapped, sensitive internal tool servers off the public internet entirely.
  7. Full Digital Lending Directions compliance for embedded BNPL/checkout credit — KFS, cooling-off, direct disbursal, CIMS/DLA registration, glass-box preference — applied without exception merely because the lending decision is embedded inside a payments or super-app flow.
  8. PA-MD-aligned merchant and settlement governance: ensure agentic payment-routing or settlement-orchestration logic only processes transactions for KYC-verified, contractually onboarded merchants, restricting split-settlement automation to the large-merchant category the PA-MD permits.
  9. Explicit liability governance for agent-made commitments: treat any payment, refund or credit commitment an agent makes as legally binding on the institution, with hard value ceilings/approval gates (echoing Section 5.4's RACI discipline) before an agent can commit autonomously.

This closes the four sector playbooks — Banking/NBFCs, Securities and Capital Markets, Insurance, and Payments/Fintech/New-Age Players — each grounded in real, documented production systems, showing how the same FREEAI Sutras, MRM lifecycle discipline and governance architecture from Chapters 2–5 must be re-calibrated against a distinct sectoral rulebook, technical latency budget and risk profile. 7.0 Data as the Substrate Everything Depends On:

Every mechanism discussed so far — FREEAI's Sutras (Chapter 3), the MRM lifecycle (Chapter 4), governance structures (Chapter 5), and the sectorspecific playbooks (Chapter 6) — ultimately operates on data. An agent cannot be fair if its training data encodes bias; it cannot be explainable if its data lineage is untraceable; it cannot be safe if its data pipeline is insecure; and it cannot be lawful if its data handling violates DPDP. Three distinct but overlapping regimes govern this substrate in Indian BFSI, and technologists must satisfy all three simultaneously:

  • DPDP Act, 2023 and DPDP Rules, 2025 — the horizontal, cross-sector law governing personal data generally, now with concrete technical mandates on retention, logging and erasure.
  • RBI's payment-data localisation regime — a sector-specific, absolute mandate for payment system data, distinct from and stricter than DPDP's more conditional cross-border approach.
  • Sectoral outsourcing, IT and cyber security frameworks (Chapter 3) — which govern how data is secured, encrypted, and audited regardless of whether AI is involved.

This chapter is organised into three sections mirroring these substrate layers: 7.1 Data Governance (this section), covering data quality, lineage, classification and lifecycle management for AI; 7.2 Privacy-Preserving and Secure AI, covering DPDP-aligned technical patterns; and 7.3 Cybersecurity and Operational Resilience, covering the security baseline AI systems must inherit from existing sectoral frameworks. We begin with data governance itself.

7.1 Data Governance for AI

7.1.1 Why Data Governance Is Different for AI Than for Traditional IT Systems

Traditional data governance in banking was built around structured, transactional data with well-understood schemas — account balances, transaction ledgers, customer master records. Agentic AI systems, by contrast, ingest and generate a fundamentally different data profile: unstructured documents (bank statements, ID proofs, damage photographs), behavioural signals (typing rhythm, device grip, transaction velocity), synthetic/derived data (embeddings, feature vectors, model outputs feeding other agents), and — critically — data whose value and risk only become apparent after model training, not at the point of collection. This is why RBI's MRM draft (Chapter 4) explicitly calls out data governance as a distinct AI risk dimension, requiring "data (empirical or synthetic)" used in AI/ML models to "conform to data governance processes" as a named lifecycle control, not an assumed background condition.

A useful conceptual frame, drawn from data-warehouse governance literature adapted for AI, organises AI data governance into five components: AI-integrated metadata management (tracking not just what data exists but which models consumed it and when), data lineage mechanisms (end-to-end visibility from raw source to model output), automated quality monitoring tailored to AI's specific failure modes (drift, label noise, feature leakage), granular access controls aligned to privacy requirements, and scalable technical infrastructure supporting the volume and velocity AI workloads demand. We examine each in turn, grounded in BFSI-specific policy and technical practice.

7.1.2 Data Classification: The Foundational Policy Layer

Before any lineage or quality control can be built, an institution must classify its data — a policy exercise every RBI cyber security and IT framework already mandates, now extended explicitly to AI training and inference data. A practical BFSI classification schema should tag every data element across at least three axes:

  • Regulatory category — payment data (RBI localisation-mandated), personal data (DPDP-governed), sector-regulated data (credit bureau, KYC, health/claims data under IRDAI), and non-regulated operational data — since each category carries a different storage, retention and cross-border rule.
  • Sensitivity tier — public, internal, confidential, and restricted/sensitive personal data (financial information, biometric data, health data), with restricted-tier data requiring the tightest access controls and encryption-at-rest-and-in-transit as a baseline.
  • AI relevance — whether the data element is used as a model input feature, training data, or purely operational/reference data — because only AI-relevant data triggers the fairness-testing and explainability obligations detailed in Chapters 4 and 6.

Technical implementation: This classification should be encoded as metadata tags at the point of ingestion, not applied retroactively — every new data source entering a feature store or data lake should be tagged with its regulatory category, sensitivity tier and AI-relevance flag as a mandatory field in the ingestion pipeline's schema, enforced by a validation gate that rejects untagged data from entering any AI-serving data layer.

7.1.3 Data Lineage: Making the Agent's Data Trail Reconstructable

Data lineage — the ability to trace a specific model output back through the exact data, transformations and model version that produced it — is the technical backbone that makes explainability (Chapter 4), adverse-action disclosure (Section 6.1), and grievance redressal (Section 6.3) actually operational rather than aspirational. Without lineage, an institution cannot answer "why was this specific customer's loan declined" beyond a generic model description.

A production-grade lineage architecture for an agentic lending or claims workflow should capture, at minimum:

  • Source-to-feature mapping: which raw data source (AA-pulled bank statement, bureau report, GST filing) fed which specific model feature, with the exact transformation logic (aggregation window, normalisation method) versioned and retrievable.
  • Feature-to-decision mapping: which feature values, combined with which model version, produced the specific score or classification for a given customer interaction — this is precisely the SHAP-based attribution layer described in Section 6.1, but it only works if the underlying feature values themselves are traceable back to source.
  • Consent-to-data mapping: for every data element used, which specific consent record (per-source, per-purpose, as required by RBI's Digital Lending Directions) authorised its collection and use — enabling an institution to prove, on audit, that a given decision only used data the customer had actually consented to for that purpose.

Technical implementation example: Modern lineage tooling (e.g., OpenLineage-compatible catalogs, or purpose-built MLOps lineage layers) tags every dataset, transformation job and model artefact with a unique identifier, and every inference call is logged with the exact input feature vector, model version hash, and output — collectively forming a queryable graph that a compliance officer or grievance handler can traverse from "customer complaint" back to "exact data and model version that produced the disputed decision," typically within minutes rather than requiring an engineering investigation.

7.1.4 Data Quality for AI: Beyond Traditional Completeness Checks

Traditional data quality management checks for completeness, accuracy and consistency. AI-specific data quality must additionally check for issues that are invisible to traditional rules but directly threaten model fairness and reliability:

  • Label quality and historical bias inheritance: if a credit-scoring model is trained on historical approval/rejection labels, and those historical decisions themselves encoded human bias (e.g., systematically harsher terms for certain geographies), the model will learn and perpetuate that bias regardless of how "clean" the data looks by traditional standards — this is why AIR/SMD fairness testing (Chapter 4, Section 6.1) must be treated as a data quality check, not merely a model-validation check.
  • Feature leakage: a feature that is only available after the outcome it's meant to predict has already occurred (e.g., a "days-past-due" field that gets populated only once a default has happened) can silently inflate a model's apparent accuracy during training while making it useless or misleading in live inference — a common, hard-to-detect failure mode in fast-moving agentic pipelines assembled from many data sources.
  • Representativeness and coverage gaps: alternative-data models (Section 6.1) trained predominantly on urban, digitally-active customer segments may perform poorly and unpredictably for rural or thin-file customers — a data quality gap that manifests as a fairness problem in production.

Technical implementation: Automated data-quality monitoring pipelines should run pre-training checks (completeness, distribution profiling, leakage detection via temporal-consistency tests) and continuous production checks (population stability index or similar drift metrics comparing live inference data against the training distribution) — feeding directly into the drift-monitoring dashboards described in Chapter 4.

7.1.5 Data Lifecycle Management: Retention, Minimisation and Erasure — Now With Binding Technical Deadlines

The DPDP Rules, 2025 move data lifecycle management from a policy aspiration to a set of concrete, auditable technical requirements:

  • Purpose-bound retention (Rule 8): Data Fiduciaries may retain personal data only as long as the specified purpose of collection is being served; once exhausted, the data must be erased — meaning an AI training dataset built for a specific model purpose cannot simply be retained indefinitely "in case it's useful later" without a fresh, documented purpose justification.
  • Mandatory security-log retention (Rule 6): system and processing logs, along with associated traffic data, must be retained for a minimum of one year for detection, investigation and remediation purposes — directly relevant to the decision-trace and audit-log requirements discussed in Chapters 4 and 6, which now have a regulatory floor on retention duration, not just an upper ceiling.
  • Automated deletion for large platforms: large online platforms must erase certain user data after a specified period of inactivity, with a mandatory 48-hour advance notification to the data principal before scheduled erasure — an engineering requirement that must be built into the data lifecycle pipeline as an automated, scheduled job with a notification trigger, not a manual periodic cleanup exercise.
  • Section 4(2) data minimisation as a field-level discipline: the DPDP Act requires personal data to be "complete, accurate, and limited to what is necessary for the purpose," and — critically — consent does not override this necessity requirement: collecting unnecessary data is a breach even with consent obtained. The practical discipline this demands is a field-by-field necessity audit of every data element feeding an AI model: for each feature, document (a) what purpose it serves, (b) whether that purpose is declared in the privacy notice, and (c) whether the purpose could be achieved without it — any field failing the necessity test should be a candidate for removal, well before the fairness or accuracy question is even asked.

Technical implementation example: A "data necessity review" gate should be added to the model-development workflow itself (Chapter 4, Stage 1): before any new feature is added to a scoring model, an automated ticket or sign-off step requires the data scientist to document the purpose, its declaration in the privacy notice, and the necessity justification — with product/compliance sign-off required before the feature can be merged into the production feature store. Retention schedules should be encoded as automated, not manual, deletion jobs: e.g., loan-application documents retained for the regulatory-mandated period post-account-closure, after which an automated job anonymises or deletes them, rather than relying on periodic manual data-hygiene exercises that are, in practice, error-prone and rarely completed on schedule.

7.1.6 Data Localisation for AI Pipelines: A Sharper, Sector-Specific Overlay on DPDP

Where DPDP's cross-border transfer regime is comparatively permissive (transfers are allowed unless a country is specifically restricted), RBI's payment data localisation mandate is absolute and sector-specific, and this distinction has direct architectural consequences for any agentic payments AI system (Section 6.4):

  • All payment system data — customer data (name, mobile, email, Aadhaar, PAN), payment-sensitive data (account details), payment credentials (OTP, PIN), and transaction data (timestamps, amounts, originating/destination system information) — must be stored only in systems located in India, with no exceptions based on company size or transaction volume.
  • Processing may occur abroad, but storage may not: if a payment-fraud-detection model's inference pipeline runs partly on overseas infrastructure (e.g., a global cloud region for a shared multinational model), the data must be deleted from foreign systems within 24 hours of processing completion, with the full dataset returned to India-only storage.
  • Annual audit and compliance certification: banks and participants must confirm adherence to data localisation at least annually via a CERT-IN empanelled auditor, with a System Audit Report submitted to the Board and then to RBI — meaning any agentic payments AI architecture must be auditable for data-residency compliance as a standing, recurring control, not a one-time architecture review.

Technical implementation: This has a direct bearing on the architectural choices described in Section 6.4 — PhonePe's Edge Framework (on-device inference) and internally air-gapped MCP servers are not merely privacy-by-design choices but also the most robust way to satisfy RBI's localisation mandate by construction, since data that never leaves the device or an Indian data centre cannot trigger a cross-border storage question at all. For any agent that must call an overseas-hosted foundation model API, institutions should architect a data-minimisation proxy layer that strips or tokenises payment-sensitive fields before the API call, ensuring only the minimum necessary, non-restricted data crosses the border, with the full restricted dataset remaining on Indian infrastructure throughout.

7.1.7 Bringing It Together: A Data Governance Maturity Checklist for AI

Before any high-risk agentic system (Chapters 4 and 6) proceeds past the development stage, its data governance foundation should satisfy five concrete tests:

  1. Is every data element feeding the model tagged with its regulatory category, sensitivity tier and AI-relevance flag at the point of ingestion — or is classification still a manual, after-the-fact exercise?
  2. Can a specific decision be traced back through feature values, model version and consent record within minutes — or does answering a customer dispute require an engineering investigation?
  3. Has every feature passed a documented necessity and leakage review before entering the production feature store — or is "more data is better" still the implicit default?
  4. Are retention and deletion schedules automated, with the DPDP-mandated one-year security-log floor and purpose-bound retention ceiling both enforced by scheduled jobs — or does data cleanup still depend on someone remembering to run it?
  5. Is payment-related data provably confined to India-located storage, with any overseas processing followed by verified, time-bound deletion — or does the institution rely on assumption rather than an annual CERT-IN-audited confirmation?

An institution that can answer all five affirmatively has built the data substrate that everything else in this report — fairness testing, explainability, human oversight, sector-specific compliance — actually depends on. Technical implementation: This classification should be encoded as metadata tags at the point of ingestion, not applied retroactively — every new data source entering a feature store or data lake should be tagged with its regulatory category, sensitivity tier and AI-relevance flag as a mandatory field in the ingestion pipeline's schema, enforced by a validation gate that rejects untagged data from entering any AI-serving data layer.

7.1.3 Data Lineage: Making the Agent's Data Trail Reconstructable

Data lineage — the ability to trace a specific model output back through the exact data, transformations and model version that produced it — is the technical backbone that makes explainability (Chapter 4), adverse-action disclosure (Section 6.1), and grievance redressal (Section 6.3) actually operational rather than aspirational. Without lineage, an institution cannot answer "why was this specific customer's loan declined" beyond a generic model description.

A production-grade lineage architecture for an agentic lending or claims workflow should capture, at minimum:

  • Source-to-feature mapping: which raw data source (AA-pulled bank statement, bureau report, GST filing) fed which specific model feature, with the exact transformation logic (aggregation window, normalisation method) versioned and retrievable.
  • Feature-to-decision mapping: which feature values, combined with which model version, produced the specific score or classification for a given customer interaction — this is precisely the SHAP-based attribution layer described in Section 6.1, but it only works if the underlying feature values themselves are traceable back to source.
  • Consent-to-data mapping: for every data element used, which specific consent record (per-source, per-purpose, as required by RBI's Digital Lending Directions) authorised its collection and use — enabling an institution to prove, on audit, that a given decision only used data the customer had actually consented to for that purpose.

Technical implementation example: Modern lineage tooling (e.g., OpenLineage-compatible catalogs, or purpose-built MLOps lineage layers) tags every dataset, transformation job and model artefact with a unique identifier, and every inference call is logged with the exact input feature vector, model version hash, and output — collectively forming a queryable graph that a compliance officer or grievance handler can traverse from "customer complaint" back to "exact data and model version that produced the disputed decision," typically within minutes rather than requiring an engineering investigation.

7.1.4 Data Quality for AI: Beyond Traditional Completeness Checks

Traditional data quality management checks for completeness, accuracy and consistency. AI-specific data quality must additionally check for issues that are invisible to traditional rules but directly threaten model fairness and reliability:

  • Label quality and historical bias inheritance: if a credit-scoring model is trained on historical approval/rejection labels, and those historical decisions themselves encoded human bias (e.g., systematically harsher terms for certain geographies), the model will learn and perpetuate that bias regardless of how "clean" the data looks by traditional standards — this is why AIR/SMD fairness testing (Chapter 4, Section 6.1) must be treated as a data quality check, not merely a model-validation check.
  • Feature leakage: a feature that is only available after the outcome it's meant to predict has already occurred (e.g., a "days-past-due" field that gets populated only once a default has happened) can silently inflate a model's apparent accuracy during training while making it useless or misleading in live inference — a common, hard-to-detect failure mode in fast-moving agentic pipelines assembled from many data sources.
  • Representativeness and coverage gaps: alternative-data models (Section 6.1) trained predominantly on urban, digitally-active customer segments may perform poorly and unpredictably for rural or thin-file customers — a data quality gap that manifests as a fairness problem in production.

Technical implementation: Automated data-quality monitoring pipelines should run pre-training checks (completeness, distribution profiling, leakage detection via temporal-consistency tests) and continuous production checks (population stability index or similar drift metrics comparing live inference data against the training distribution) — feeding directly into the drift-monitoring dashboards described in Chapter 4.

7.1.5 Data Lifecycle Management: Retention, Minimisation and Erasure — Now With Binding Technical Deadlines

The DPDP Rules, 2025 move data lifecycle management from a policy aspiration to a set of concrete, auditable technical requirements:

  • Purpose-bound retention (Rule 8): Data Fiduciaries may retain personal data only as long as the specified purpose of collection is being served; once exhausted, the data must be erased — meaning an AI training dataset built for a specific model purpose cannot simply be retained indefinitely "in case it's useful later" without a fresh, documented purpose justification.
  • Mandatory security-log retention (Rule 6): system and processing logs, along with associated traffic data, must be retained for a minimum of one year for detection, investigation and remediation purposes — directly relevant to the decision-trace and audit-log requirements discussed in Chapters 4 and 6, which now have a regulatory floor on retention duration, not just an upper ceiling.
  • Automated deletion for large platforms: large online platforms must erase certain user data after a specified period of inactivity, with a mandatory 48-hour advance notification to the data principal before scheduled erasure — an engineering requirement that must be built into the data lifecycle pipeline as an automated, scheduled job with a notification trigger, not a manual periodic cleanup exercise.
  • Section 4(2) data minimisation as a field-level discipline: the DPDP Act requires personal data to be "complete, accurate, and limited to what is necessary for the purpose," and — critically — consent does not override this necessity requirement: collecting unnecessary data is a breach even with consent obtained. The practical discipline this demands is a field-by-field necessity audit of every data element feeding an AI model: for each feature, document (a) what purpose it serves, (b) whether that purpose is declared in the privacy notice, and (c) whether the purpose could be achieved without it — any field failing the necessity test should be a candidate for removal, well before the fairness or accuracy question is even asked.

Technical implementation example: A "data necessity review" gate should be added to the model-development workflow itself (Chapter 4, Stage 1): before any new feature is added to a scoring model, an automated ticket or sign-off step requires the data scientist to document the purpose, its declaration in the privacy notice, and the necessity justification — with product/compliance sign-off required before the feature can be merged into the production feature store. Retention schedules should be encoded as automated, not manual, deletion jobs: e.g., loan-application documents retained for the regulatory-mandated period post-account-closure, after which an automated job anonymises or deletes them, rather than relying on periodic manual data-hygiene exercises that are, in practice, error-prone and rarely completed on schedule.

7.1.6 Data Localisation for AI Pipelines: A Sharper, Sector-Specific Overlay on DPDP

Where DPDP's cross-border transfer regime is comparatively permissive (transfers are allowed unless a country is specifically restricted), RBI's payment data localisation mandate is absolute and sector-specific, and this distinction has direct architectural consequences for any agentic payments AI system (Section 6.4):

  • All payment system data — customer data (name, mobile, email, Aadhaar, PAN), payment-sensitive data (account details), payment credentials (OTP, PIN), and transaction data (timestamps, amounts, originating/destination system information) — must be stored only in systems located in India, with no exceptions based on company size or transaction volume.
  • Processing may occur abroad, but storage may not: if a payment-fraud-detection model's inference pipeline runs partly on overseas infrastructure (e.g., a global cloud region for a shared multinational model), the data must be deleted from foreign systems within 24 hours of processing completion, with the full dataset returned to India-only storage.
  • Annual audit and compliance certification: banks and participants must confirm adherence to data localisation at least annually via a CERT-IN empanelled auditor, with a System Audit Report submitted to the Board and then to RBI — meaning any agentic payments AI architecture must be auditable for data-residency compliance as a standing, recurring control, not a one-time architecture review.

Technical implementation: This has a direct bearing on the architectural choices described in Section 6.4 — PhonePe's Edge Framework (on-device inference) and internally air-gapped MCP servers are not merely privacy-by-design choices but also the most robust way to satisfy RBI's localisation mandate by construction, since data that never leaves the device or an Indian data centre cannot trigger a cross-border storage question at all. For any agent that must call an overseas-hosted foundation model API, institutions should architect a data-minimisation proxy layer that strips or tokenises payment-sensitive fields before the API call, ensuring only the minimum necessary, non-restricted data crosses the border, with the full restricted dataset remaining on Indian infrastructure throughout.

7.1.7 Bringing It Together: A Data Governance Maturity Checklist for AI

Before any high-risk agentic system (Chapters 4 and 6) proceeds past the development stage, its data governance foundation should satisfy five concrete tests:

  1. Is every data element feeding the model tagged with its regulatory category, sensitivity tier and AI-relevance flag at the point of ingestion — or is classification still a manual, after-the-fact exercise?
  2. Can a specific decision be traced back through feature values, model version and consent record within minutes — or does answering a customer dispute require an engineering investigation?
  3. Has every feature passed a documented necessity and leakage review before entering the production feature store — or is "more data is better" still the implicit default?
  4. Are retention and deletion schedules automated, with the DPDP-mandated one-year security-log floor and purpose-bound retention ceiling both enforced by scheduled jobs — or does data cleanup still depend on someone remembering to run it?
  5. Is payment-related data provably confined to India-located storage, with any overseas processing followed by verified, time-bound deletion — or does the institution rely on assumption rather than an annual CERT-IN-audited confirmation?

An institution that can answer all five affirmatively has built the data substrate that everything else in this report — fairness testing, explainability, human oversight, sector-specific compliance — actually depends on. 7.2 Privacy-Preserving and Secure AI

7.2.1 Why Privacy Engineering, Not Just Privacy Policy, Is Now Required

Section 7.1 established that DPDP obligations (necessity, minimisation, retention, deletion) must be built into data pipelines as automated technical controls. Privacy-preserving AI takes this one step further: it asks whether the AI system itself — the model architecture, the training process, the inference call — can be engineered so that privacy is mathematically or architecturally guaranteed, rather than merely governed by policy and access control. This distinction matters because access controls answer "who is authorised to see this data," but agentic AI raises a harder question: even authorised model training or cross-institutional model sharing can leak individual customer information through the model itself — via membership-inference attacks (determining whether a specific person's data was in the training set) or model-inversion attacks (reconstructing approximate training records from model outputs). Privacy-preserving AI techniques exist precisely to close this gap.

Four technique families are directly relevant to BFSI agentic AI: pseudonymisation and tokenisation (the baseline, most widely deployed), federated learning (data never leaves its source), differential privacy (mathematically bounded information leakage), and homomorphic encryption / secure enclaves (computation on data that is never decrypted). We examine each with regulatory grounding and technical depth.

7.2.2 Pseudonymisation and Tokenisation: The Baseline Control

Policy grounding: DPDP does not mandate pseudonymisation by name, but Rule 6's security-safeguard requirements and the Act's "reasonable security safeguards" standard are widely interpreted as requiring exactly this class of control for any restricted-tier data (Section 7.1) used in AI pipelines — and RBI's cyber security and outsourcing frameworks separately require encryption and access segregation for sensitive customer data regardless of AI involvement.

Technical concept: Pseudonymisation replaces direct identifiers (name, PAN, account number) with a reversible token, keeping the mapping table in a separate, tightly access-controlled vault — so a data scientist building a fraud model can work with "Customer_Token_88291" rather than the real PAN, and re-identification is only possible for those with vault access, not for the model itself or anyone querying its outputs. Tokenisation is the payments-industry-specific variant: RBI's own card-tokenisation mandate for e-commerce replaces actual card numbers with merchant-specific tokens, and the same architectural principle — irreversible-without-a-separate-secret-mapping — extends naturally to AI feature pipelines.

Worked example — IDPIC's hash-before-transmission pattern (Section 6.4): every participating bank hashes a customer's phone number or account identifier locally, before it ever leaves the institution, using a shared hashing scheme (e.g., SHA-256 with a common salt), so the central Smart Registry only ever stores and matches on fixed-length hashed strings — enabling cross-institutional matching without any institution seeing another's raw customer data. This is a directly reusable pattern: any BFSI institution building a shared or federated AI system with peer institutions should tokenise/hash all cross-institutional identifiers at the point of origin, never centrally.

Limitation to note explicitly: pseudonymisation alone does not defeat re-identification via combination with other data (the "mosaic effect") — a tokenised dataset that retains rich behavioural features (transaction timing, geolocation, device fingerprint) can often still be re-identified by cross-referencing with other available datasets, which is precisely why higher-assurance techniques are needed for the highest-risk AI use cases.

7.2.3 Federated Learning: Keeping Data at the Source

Concept: Federated learning (FL) inverts the traditional ML pipeline — instead of centralising data and training one model, each institution trains a local model on its own data, and only the resulting model updates (gradients or weights, not raw data) are sent to a central aggregator, which combines them into an improved global model that is then redistributed. No raw customer data ever leaves the institution's own infrastructure — directly satisfying both DPDP's minimisation principle and RBI's payment-data localisation mandate (Section 7.1) by construction, since the underlying restricted data physically never crosses institutional or national boundaries.

Why BFSI specifically benefits: fraud and credit-risk patterns vary across institutions, but share common underlying characteristics — a consortium of banks can build a materially more robust fraud model collaboratively than any single institution could alone, without ever pooling raw transaction data.

Documented real-world outcomes: a consortium fraud-detection FL deployment, benchmarked over 12 months post-implementation, showed consortium-wide fraud-detection accuracy improving by 18–34% to reach 86%, false-positive rates improving 126% (to roughly 1:520), cross-border fraud detection improving by 41%, new-model deployment time cut by 75% (from months to 4–6 weeks), smaller banks achieving detection rates approaching those of larger institutions (a notable equity benefit — smaller/regional banks that individually lack enough fraud examples to train a robust model benefit disproportionately from the shared signal), and annual fraud losses across the consortium reduced by an estimated €28.7 million — all while no raw data left any institutional boundary, satisfying regulatory compliance by architecture rather than by audit trail alone.

Technical architecture used in that deployment: a central server hosted by a neutral third party (directly analogous to IDPIC's role in Section 6.4), local model training at each bank on proprietary data, secure aggregation using homomorphic encryption for the update-combination step itself, gradient quantisation reducing communication overhead by 78%, differential privacy applied to updates at a privacy budget of ε=3.0, and personalised federated learning allowing each institution to retain regional/segment-specific model customisation rather than forcing a single undifferentiated global model on all participants.

Caveat — FL is not automatically privacy-safe: federated learning alone only prevents raw-data sharing; model updates themselves can still leak information through model-inversion or membership-inference attacks unless combined with differential privacy or secure aggregation — which is why production-grade FL deployments in finance layer DP and/or homomorphic encryption on top of FL rather than treating FL as a complete solution on its own.

7.2.4 Differential Privacy: A Mathematical Guarantee, Not Just an Engineering Pattern

Concept, defined precisely: Differential Privacy (DP), introduced by Cynthia Dwork and colleagues, provides a formal mathematical guarantee that a computation's output does not depend too much on any single individual's data. Formally, a randomised mechanism M is (ε, δ)-differentially private if, for any two datasets differing in exactly one record, the probability of any given output changes by at most a factor of eϵeϵ (plus a small failure probability δ). The parameter ε (epsilon) is the "privacy budget" — smaller values mean stronger privacy but more noise (and therefore less model accuracy); this is a genuine, quantifiable trade-off that a BFSI institution's risk and compliance function should sign off on explicitly, not something a data science team should set unilaterally.

Technical implementation — DP-SGD (Differentially Private Stochastic Gradient Descent): the standard mechanism for training neural networks under DP clips each training example's individual gradient contribution to a maximum norm (bounding how much any single customer's data can influence the model), then adds calibrated Gaussian or Laplace noise to the aggregated gradient before each parameter update — with a "privacy accountant" tracking cumulative privacy loss across all training steps via composition theorems, since each additional training round consumes more of the fixed privacy budget.

Documented privacy-utility trade-off, quantified: across studies applying DP to real banking data (loan approval, credit-risk models), the empirical pattern is consistent: at ε=1 (strong privacy), accuracy degrades by roughly 5%; at ε=5 (moderate privacy), the loss falls to roughly 1%; a federated credit-risk study similarly found DP-enabled FL with ε=8.65 delivered "the most balanced" outcome — 87–90% accuracy with enforceable privacy guarantees and moderate computational cost, compared to ~91% for a no-privacy baseline and ~90% for a homomorphic-encryption variant. This gives a BFSI institution's model-risk team a concrete decision framework: ε=1–3 for highly sensitive, externally-shared or regulator-scrutinised models; ε=5–10 for internal models with moderate privacy requirements, calibrated against the specific accuracy loss the business can tolerate for that use case.

When to use DP, stated as a decision rule: add differential privacy when model outputs will be released, queried, or made accessible to parties outside the training pipeline; when the threat model includes membership-inference or model-inversion attacks; or when regulatory or contractual obligations require a formal, provable privacy guarantee rather than a best-effort control. Conversely, for models that remain entirely internal with strong perimeter access controls and no external query surface, DP's accuracy cost may not be justified — this regime can often be adequately satisfied through perimeter controls alone.

BFSI-specific application — synthetic data for AML/fraud model development: differentially-private synthetic data generation (using GANs or VAEs enhanced with DP mechanisms) allows an institution to generate realistic, statistically representative transaction datasets for AML and fraud-model development and testing, without any risk that the synthetic records could be reverse-engineered to identify a real customer — directly solving Chapter 4's "no synthetic data for real quantitative claims" concern in the opposite direction, i.e., using DP-synthetic data specifically for safe model development and testing, not for reporting real findings.

7.2.5 Homomorphic Encryption: Computing on Data That Is Never Decrypted

Concept: Homomorphic encryption (HE) allows mathematical operations (addition, multiplication) to be performed directly on encrypted data, producing an encrypted result that, when decrypted, matches what the operation would have produced on the plaintext — meaning a service provider or cloud host can run a computation without ever seeing the underlying data in readable form.

Worked example — Mastercard's cross-border fraud-check pilot: working with Singapore's Infocomm Media Development Authority, Mastercard piloted a system to check whether a given International Bank Account Number (IBAN) has a high fraud-risk score, without transferring the IBAN itself across jurisdictions. The architecture: the querying entity encrypts the IBAN using its own public key and submits it to a secure query "hub"; the hub distributes the encrypted query to source entities (local risk-score holders) in each jurisdiction; each source entity performs homomorphic operations directly on the encrypted IBAN to check it against local records, returning only an encrypted TRUE/FALSE result (never decrypting the query, never revealing its own underlying risk-score data); the hub aggregates the encrypted responses; and the original querying entity decrypts the final aggregated result using its private key. The result: a querying entity learns only "flagged somewhere, yes/no" — not which jurisdiction flagged it, not the underlying risk score, and the source entities never see the actual IBAN being queried — solving cross-border data-transfer and localisation restrictions simultaneously while still enabling meaningful fraud collaboration.

Why this matters directly for Indian BFSI's localisation regime (Section 7.1): HE offers a structural answer to the tension between RBI's absolute payment-data localisation mandate and the operational need to check risk signals against overseas or cross-institutional data sources — since the underlying restricted data is never decrypted outside its home jurisdiction, only an encrypted query and an encrypted boolean response cross the border, a pattern directly analogous to what IDPIC's hashing approach achieves for domestic cross-bank sharing (Section 7.2.2) but extended to genuine mathematical computation rather than simple hash-matching.

Practical caveat: HE carries substantial computational overhead compared to plaintext processing — production financial deployments (Mastercard's pilot, HE-enabled federated credit models) report accuracy preserved at near-parity with unencrypted baselines (e.g., ~90% vs ~91% in one federated credit-risk study) but at meaningfully increased latency and compute cost, meaning HE is currently best suited to lower-frequency, higher-sensitivity cross-jurisdictional queries rather than the sub-200-millisecond, billions-of-transactions-per-month UPI fraud-scoring path described in Section 6.4.

7.2.6 A Decision Framework: Choosing the Right Privacy-Preserving Technique

Rather than treating these four techniques as competing alternatives, BFSI institutions should apply them as a layered stack matched to specific risk scenarios:

7.2.7 Governance Overlay: Privacy-Preserving Techniques Do Not Replace Chapter 4/5 Controls

A critical discipline point: none of these techniques substitute for the model-risk-management lifecycle (Chapter 4) or the governance/accountability structures (Chapter 5) — they are complementary, not alternative, controls. A federated fraud model still requires fairness testing (each institution's local data may carry different demographic skews that a shared global model could inadvertently amplify); a differentially-private model still requires human-in-the-loop review for high-impact BFSI decisions (Section 6.1–6.4); and the choice of ε, the FL aggregation architecture, or the HE query-hub design should all pass through the same model-registration, sign-off and audit-trail discipline as any other high-risk AI system. Institutions should also explicitly document, in the vendor-governance contracts discussed in Chapter 5, which privacy-preserving techniques a third-party AI vendor uses internally — since Regulation 16C-style sole-liability principles (Section 6.2) mean the regulated institution cannot outsource its accountability for privacy failures merely because the underlying cryptographic technique was implemented by a vendor.

7.3 Cybersecurity and Operational Resilience

7.3.1 Why AI Requires an Extension, Not a Replacement, of Existing Cyber Frameworks

RBI's existing cyber security and IT frameworks — the Cyber Security Framework for Banks, the IT Framework for NBFCs, and associated resilience directions (Chapter 3) — already mandate board-approved security policies, incident response, and continuous monitoring. Agentic AI does not require an entirely new regulatory apparatus, but it does introduce a genuinely new attack surface that these existing frameworks were not written to anticipate: models that can be poisoned rather than merely breached, agents that can be manipulated through the data they read rather than the credentials they hold, and multi-agent systems where a single compromised component can cascade failure across an entire workflow. This section maps that new attack surface against existing regulatory expectations and translates it into concrete technical controls.

7.3.2 The Expanded Threat Taxonomy: From Model-Level to Agent-Level Risk

Security risk in AI systems has evolved through four distinct generations, and Indian BFSI institutions deploying agentic systems today must defend against all four simultaneously, not just the most recent one:

  • Traditional cybersecurity risks — data theft, phishing, ransomware, denial-of-service — remain fully applicable to any AI system's underlying infrastructure.
  • Predictive AI/ML-era risks — data poisoning and algorithmic bias (training-time attacks) and model evasion in production (inference-time attacks) — directly relevant to the fraud-scoring and credit-scoring models discussed throughout Chapter 6.
  • Generative AI/LLM-era risks — catalogued in the OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications (2025): prompt injection (LLM01), sensitive information disclosure (LLM02), supply chain vulnerabilities (LLM05), system prompt leakage (LLM07), excessive agency (LLM08), and model theft (LLM10).
  • Agentic AI-era risks — catalogued in the newly released OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications (2026): risks that emerge specifically because an agent has goals, memory, tool access and delegated authority, not merely input/output behaviour.

The OWASP Agentic Top 10 (ASI01–ASI10) is the most directly relevant framework for the agentic systems described throughout Chapter 6, and BFSI security teams should treat it as a standing checklist against every agent before production release:

7.3.3 A Documented Real-World Incident: Prompt Injection at a European Digital Bank

A concrete, publicly documented case illustrates ASI01/LLM01 risk with unusual precision and is directly instructive for any BFSI institution deploying a conversational or agentic banking assistant. Security researchers (Blue41) demonstrated an indirect prompt injection attack against Bunq — Europe's second-largest digital bank, with over 20 million customers — for a total attacker cost of €0.02. The attack chain: (1) the attacker sends a trivial-value SEPA transfer to the victim, embedding a prompt-injection payload inside the free-form transaction description field; (2) the victim later asks their banking app's AI assistant a routine question (e.g., "show me my recent transactions"); (3) the assistant retrieves transaction records — including the attacker-controlled description — and places them directly into the LLM's context window as background data; (4) because the model cannot structurally distinguish "instructions from the system prompt" from "data retrieved from the database" — both are processed as tokens — the injected text was interpreted as an instruction, and the assistant was manipulated into generating a convincing phishing message inside the bank's own trusted interface, referencing the victim's real account context and making it far more credible than any external phishing attempt.

This incident crystallises the core technical lesson for every agentic system described in Chapter 6: any data field an agent reads — a transaction memo, a claims description, a chat message, a document upload — is a potential instruction-injection vector, and the danger is highest precisely where institutions have the least visibility, because the attacker never needs direct access to the target system at all; they only need to place text somewhere the agent will eventually retrieve.

7.3.4 Technical Defences: A Layered Model, With Explicit Limits on Each Layer

No single control eliminates prompt-injection or agentic-manipulation risk; defence-in-depth is the only credible posture, and institutions should understand precisely what each layer stops and does not stop:

  • Context minimisation — pass only the data fields the current task genuinely requires into the LLM's context (e.g., a balance-check query should never pull transaction-description fields at all); stops injections in fields not retrieved, but does not stop injections in fields the task legitimately needs.
  • Structural data/instruction separation — wrap all retrieved content in explicit delimiters (e.g., <retrieved-data source="transactions" trust="untrusted">…</retrieved-data>) and instruct the system prompt to treat everything inside such tags as data to reference, never as instructions to follow; stops naive payloads and reduces injection probability, but does not stop well-crafted payloads exploiting model ambiguity.
  • Output and action allowlisting — constrain what the agent is structurally permitted to output or execute (e.g., an agent with no capability to initiate outbound transfers cannot act on an injected transfer instruction, regardless of whether the injection succeeds); stops unauthorised tool calls and link exfiltration, but does not stop text manipulation or user deception within permitted outputs.
  • Human confirmation for high-impact actions — any action with real-world financial consequence (money movement, record mutation, communication dispatch) must require explicit human confirmation displaying the full proposed action in plain, system-derived language — never the LLM's own summary of what it intends to do, since a compromised agent could equally craft a misleading summary of its own malicious action. This directly operationalises the human-in-the-loop discipline established in Chapters 4 and 6 as a security control, not merely a governance one.
  • Runtime behavioural monitoring — build behavioural baselines of normal agent operation (typical tool-call sequences, typical transaction-value ranges, typical query patterns) and flag statistical deviations in real time, catching manipulation that bypasses preventive controls entirely — the same anomaly-detection principle already used for fraud (Section 6.3/6.4), now turned inward on the agent's own behaviour.
  • Least-privilege tool access — scope every agent's tool permissions to the minimum required for its specific task (echoing ASI02/ASI03), so that even a successfully injected instruction has no execution path to a capability the agent was never granted.

7.3.5 Data and Model Poisoning: The Training-Time Threat

Separately from prompt injection, BFSI fraud and credit models face poisoning attacks at the training-data layer: an adversary generates synthetic fraudulent transactions, tests them against a surrogate model ("Oracle") approximating the target fraud-detection system to find transactions that evade detection, then — where possible — seeds these evasive patterns into the training data pipeline, shifting the deployed model's decision boundary to systematically miss a specific fraud pattern. Documented research demonstrates that shallower, more interpretable models (logistic regression, simple neural networks, SVM) are more vulnerable to this evasion technique than deeper models, with attackers extracting the highest financial gain specifically from SVM-based fraud classifiers — a finding with direct relevance to Section 6.1's emphasis on "glass-box" models for regulatory transparency, since there is a genuine trade-off between model interpretability (regulatory preference) and adversarial robustness (security preference) that risk and compliance functions must consciously weigh, not assume away. The documented countermeasure — adversarial training, where artificial fraud examples generated by perturbing legitimate transactions are deliberately included in the training set with correct labels, teaching the model to recognise evasive patterns — reduced stolen-amount outcomes by up to 100% in controlled experiments, and should be treated as a standard hardening step for any production fraud-detection model, not an optional research technique.

7.3.6 Agentic Supply Chain Security: A New Governance Surface

ASI04 (Agentic Supply Chain Vulnerabilities) deserves particular emphasis given Chapter 5's vendor-governance discussion and Chapter 6's repeated reliance on third-party LSPs, algo providers and insurtech platforms. The recommended technical control is an AI Bill of Materials (AIBOM) — a structured, auditable inventory of every model, dataset, plugin, tool and third-party component an agentic system depends on, with cryptographic signing and provenance tracking for each component — directly analogous to a software bill of materials (SBOM) in traditional application security, but extended to cover training data lineage and model version provenance as well. Combined with Regulation 16C's sole-liability principle (Section 6.2) and the LSP-accountability rule in digital lending (Section 6.1), this means a regulated BFSI institution's security function must be able to answer, on demand, precisely which third-party models and data sources feed every production agent — not merely which vendors it has contracted with.

7.3.7 Operational Resilience: Extending RBI's Existing Baseline to Agentic Systems

Bringing this together with RBI's existing cyber security and IT frameworks, a responsibly deployed agentic AI system in BFSI should extend the institution's board-approved cyber policy with AI-specific additions:

  • Pre-deployment red-teaming against the full OWASP Agentic Top 10 checklist, not merely traditional penetration testing, before any agent with financial-action capability (payments, disbursals, claims settlement) goes live.
  • Continuous adversarial-robustness testing for fraud and credit-scoring models, incorporating adversarial training against synthetically generated evasive transaction patterns as a standing model-validation step (Chapter 4), not a one-time audit.
  • AIBOM maintenance and signed-component verification for every agentic system, integrated into the existing vendor-risk-management process (Chapter 5) rather than run as a parallel, disconnected security exercise.
  • Blast-radius isolation for multi-agent architectures — architecting fraud, claims and underwriting agent pipelines (Sections 6.1–6.4) so that a single compromised or malfunctioning agent cannot cascade failure across the entire workflow, via circuit breakers and bounded inter-agent trust.
  • Behavioural-baseline monitoring and kill-switch readiness for every autonomous agent, interoperating with (not conflicting against) exchange-level and NPCI-level kill-switch authority already established in Sections 6.2 and 6.4.
  • Mandatory human confirmation, displaying raw system-derived action detail, for every financially consequential agent action — the single most consistently recommended control across every real-world incident and framework examined in this section.

Chapter Summary

Data governance, privacy-preserving engineering, and cybersecurity are not three separate compliance workstreams — they are three layers of the same substrate on which every fairness test, explainability claim, and human-oversight control examined in Chapters 4 through 6 ultimately depends. A model cannot be fair if its data lineage cannot be traced; it cannot be DPDP-compliant if privacy is bolted on rather than engineered in; and it cannot be trusted with financial autonomy if its attack surface — from prompt injection to agentic supply chain compromise — remains unmapped. The institutions best positioned for RAI readiness will be those that treat this chapter's controls as foundational infrastructure, built once and reused across every sector playbook in Chapter 6, rather than as a bespoke afterthought for each new agentic use case. Chapter 8 now turns to how these foundations are validated and assured on an ongoing basis — model risk management, independent validation, and audit — closing the loop from data substrate to institutional accountability.

Chapter 8 – Model Risk Management, Validation and Assurance

8.0 Context Setting: From Foundational Substrate to Institutional Assurance

Chapter 7 established the data, privacy and security substrate on which every AI system depends. This chapter addresses the layer built on top of that substrate: the formal governance discipline that determines whether a model is fit to be trusted with financial decisions in the first place, kept fit throughout its operational life, and retired safely when it is not. This is no longer an optional best practice in India — it is now the subject of the most consequential piece of AI-specific regulation RBI has issued to date.

On 24 June 2026, RBI released its Draft Guidance on Regulatory Principles for Model Risk Management (Press Release 2026-2027/528) for public consultation, with comments due by 24 July 2026. This is the single most detailed, India-specific, AI-inclusive MRM framework issued to date, and it deserves to be read as the operative regulatory backbone for this entire chapter — sitting alongside, and giving binding teeth to, the FREEAI Sutras (Chapter 3). The guidance applies across 11 Regulated Entity categories and organises MRM around four pillars: model inventory and classification, lifecycle management including validation, continuous oversight and monitoring, and governance, third-party accountability and AI-specific controls. This chapter follows that structure, beginning with the foundational pillar — inventory and classification — since nothing downstream (validation, monitoring, governance) is possible without it.

8.1 Model Inventory and Classification

8.1.1 Why This Is the Foundational Pillar: "No Record, No Deployment"

Every subsequent MRM control — validation, monitoring, kill switches, RMCB oversight — depends on a single precondition being satisfied first: the institution must know, with complete and current accuracy, which models exist, what they do, and how much they matter. RBI's draft guidance makes this an explicit, binding principle: a model cannot be developed, deployed, or relied upon unless it is first formally recorded in the enterprise model inventory. This is a meaningfully higher bar than most Indian BFSI institutions' current practice, where AI/ML tools are frequently adopted by individual business units, embedded in vendor platforms, or built as "shadow" analytics scripts outside any centrally visible register.

8.1.2 Policy Requirement: What Counts as a "Model"

The regulatory definition of "model" is deliberately expansive, and getting this definition right is the single most consequential early decision an institution's RAI programme will make. RBI defines a model as any system that "takes inputs, applies processing logic, and produces outputs that materially affect business decisions" — explicitly extending to "algorithms, analytics, interfaces, applications, decision-based rules, computational tools," regardless of what the institution internally calls the system. This means a spreadsheet-based credit calculator, a legacy rules engine, a third-party fraud-scoring plug-in, and a frontier LLM-based underwriting agent (Section 6.1's Cyclops/Helios stack) all fall under the same regulatory definition — differentiated only by risk tier, never by exemption from the inventory obligation itself.

Practical implication: institutions cannot rely on their existing "AI/ML register" (typically built for SEBI-style AI/ML reporting, Section 6.2) as a substitute for this broader inventory — a full model-discovery exercise is required, one that will likely surface spreadsheet tools, business-rules engines, and shadow-IT analytics scripts that were never previously classified as "models" at all.

8.1.3 Mandatory Inventory Metadata: The Technical Schema

RBI's draft specifies that the inventory must be continuously updated and must cover models across their full lifecycle status — active, under development, inactive, and decommissioned — with defined, mandatory metadata fields for each entry:

  • Owner — the business unit or individual accountable for the model's use and outcomes.
  • Developer — who built the model (internal team or named third-party vendor).
  • Validator — the independent function that reviewed the model (must be organisationally separate from development, per Section 8.1.5 below).
  • Approver — who authorised the model for production use, calibrated to its risk tier.
  • Risk tier — the classification described in Section 8.1.4.
  • Intended use — the specific business decision the model supports, and explicitly, the boundaries of what it should not be used for.
  • Upstream/downstream dependencies — which data sources feed the model, and which other systems or agents consume its output (directly connecting this inventory to the data-lineage discipline of Section 7.1).
  • Key observations — a running log of findings from validation, monitoring, and audit activity.

Technical implementation example: a production-grade model inventory is typically built as a structured metadata catalog (extending the same lineage/catalog tooling discussed in Section 7.1.3) rather than a static spreadsheet, with each model entry assigned a unique, versioned identifier that persists across model updates — so that a validation finding, an audit observation, or a customer complaint can be traced to the exact model version in production at the time, not merely to "the credit scoring model" in the abstract. Decommissioned models, along with their full lifecycle documentation, must be retained for a minimum of ten years — meaning the inventory architecture must support long-term, immutable archival of retired model records, not just live-model tracking.

8.1.4 Risk-Based Tiering: Concepts and the AI-Specific Extension

Traditional tiering factors. Conventional MRM practice (and RBI's draft, in its non-AI provisions) tiers models by two familiar dimensions: complexity (how sophisticated the underlying methodology is, and how difficult it is to interpret or challenge) and materiality (how significant the model's decisions are to the institution's financial position or to customer outcomes). A simple, well-understood interest-calculation formula sits in a low tier; a portfolio-level economic-capital model sits in a high tier — the same logic long applied to traditional statistical and actuarial models.

The AI-specific extension — reliance and autonomy. RBI's draft adds two further tiering factors that apply specifically to AI/ML models, and this is one of the more genuinely novel aspects of the guidance relative to international precedent (Section 8.2 discusses this comparison in depth):

  • Reliance — the degree to which the institution's actual decision depends on the AI model's output, as opposed to using the output as one input among several considered by a human decision-maker.
  • Autonomy — the degree to which the model can act independently, without human intervention, before its output takes effect.

The practical consequence is that two models of similar statistical complexity can sit in different risk tiers purely because one operates with materially more autonomous decision-making authority. For example: a credit-scoring model that generates a risk score for a human underwriter's consideration (Section 6.1's earlier-generation practice) may sit in a moderate tier; the same underlying scoring logic embedded in an agentic system like Project Helios, where the agent auto-approves or auto-declines within defined parameters with no human touchpoint (Section 6.1.1), should sit in a materially higher tier — not because the mathematics changed, but because reliance and autonomy did.

Technical implementation — a worked scoring approach: institutions should build a documented, repeatable tiering rubric rather than relying on ad hoc judgment calls. A practical four-factor scoring matrix (complexity, materiality, reliance, autonomy — each scored on a defined 1–4 scale with explicit criteria) produces a composite score mapped to a small number of discrete tiers (e.g., Tier 1–Low, Tier 2–Moderate, Tier 3–High, Tier 4–Critical), with any model scoring "high" or above on either reliance or autonomy automatically escalated by at least one tier regardless of its complexity/materiality score alone — ensuring a highly autonomous but "simple" agent cannot be under-tiered simply because its underlying algorithm is not statistically sophisticated.

Governance consequence of tiering. High-risk models require RMCB (Risk Management Committee of the Board) approval before deployment — they cannot be cleared by the technology or model-development team alone. The tiering methodology itself must be reviewed by the Board at least annually, and the tiering outcomes (i.e., which specific models sit in which tier) must be reviewed by the RMCB at least annually — a deliberate two-tier governance split, with the Board setting the framework and the RMCB applying it to the live inventory.

8.1.5 Classification Governance: Independence as a Structural Requirement

RBI's draft codifies a specific organisational separation as a mandatory structure, not merely good practice: the Model Validator function must be independent of model development, ownership, or use. This directly echoes the "second line of defence" principle long established in international MRM practice — most notably the Federal Reserve/OCC's SR 11-7 framework, which built its entire model-governance pillar around the concept of independent, empowered "effective challenge" to model developers and owners. For classification specifically, this means the same team that builds or champions a model (e.g., the data science team behind an agentic underwriting system) cannot be the same team that assigns its risk tier or signs off on its inventory entry — that classification judgment must sit with an organisationally separate validation function reporting through the RMCB.

8.1.6 Connecting Inventory and Classification to the Sector Playbooks

The inventory-and-tiering discipline established here gives concrete institutional form to the agent-registration recommendations made throughout Chapter 6's sector playbooks:

  • Every production agent in the Cyclops/Helios/Nostradamus lending stack (Section 6.1) must sit in this single, RMCB-visible inventory, tagged with its own risk tier, reliance and autonomy scores — not bundled together as one undifferentiated "AI lending system" entry.
  • SEBI's Black Box vs. White Box algo classification (Section 6.2) and RBI's reliance/autonomy tiering are conceptually parallel but institutionally distinct exercises — an institution should map its SEBI algo-registration category to its internal RBI-style risk tier explicitly, since a Black Box algo will almost always warrant a high internal tier, but the two classification systems serve different regulators and cannot be assumed interchangeable.
  • The multi-agent fraud and claims stack in insurance (Section 6.3) — Voice Agent, Document Agent, Data Agent, and the meta-model combining their scores — should each appear as separate inventory entries with their own dependencies mapped, not as a single opaque "fraud system," precisely so that a validation finding against one component agent doesn't get lost inside an aggregate system-level record.
  • PhonePe's Guardrails four-layer architecture (Section 6.4) illustrates the dependency-mapping requirement concretely: the Connected Intelligence, Action Intelligence, Profile Intelligence and Behavioural Biometrics layers all feed a combined risk decision, and an accurate inventory must capture this exact upstream/downstream dependency chain so that a change to one layer's model triggers a documented review of its effect on the combined output.

8.1.7 A Readiness Checklist for Building the Inventory

Before proceeding to validation (Section 8.2) and monitoring (Section 8.3), institutions should be able to answer affirmatively on five points:

  1. Has a full model-discovery exercise been run using RBI's expansive definition of "model" — not the institution's current, narrower AI/ML register — to surface spreadsheet tools, rules engines and shadow-IT analytics that likely qualify?
  2. Does every inventory entry carry the complete mandatory metadata set (owner, developer, validator, approver, risk tier, intended use, dependencies, key observations)?
  3. Has a documented, repeatable tiering rubric been built — incorporating complexity, materiality, reliance and autonomy as four distinct scored factors — rather than relying on ad hoc classification judgment calls?
  4. Is the Model Validator function organisationally separate from every model-development and model-owning team whose models it classifies and reviews?
  5. Is there a defined, auditable process for the Board to review tiering methodology and the RMCB to review tiering outcomes, each at least annually?

An institution that can answer all five affirmatively has the foundation required for meaningful independent validation — the subject of the next section, 8.2, which examines what RBI's draft guidance requires once a model has been correctly inventoried and tiered.

8.2 Validation and Independent Review

8.2.1 Why Validation Must Be Structurally Independent

Inventory and classification (Section 8.1) tell an institution what models exist and how much they matter. Validation answers the harder question: can this specific model be trusted to do what it claims to do, for as long as it remains in production? RBI's draft guidance treats this as a distinct, non-negotiable discipline requiring a dedicated organisational function — the Model Validator — defined explicitly as independent of model development, ownership, or use. This is the same "second line of defence" principle that has anchored international MRM practice since the Federal Reserve/OCC's SR 11-7 (2011), which built its entire governance pillar around empowered, independent "effective challenge" to model developers. The rationale is straightforward: a team that built or champions a model has an inherent incentive — however unconscious — to interpret ambiguous evidence in the model's favour; validation only has integrity if the reviewing function has no stake in the model's continuation.

8.2.2 Scope: All Models, Including Vendor and Third-Party Models

A critical policy point, easily overlooked: RBI's draft is explicit that vendor certification is not sufficient on its own — every model, whether built internally or procured from a third party, must undergo independent validation performed by the RE itself. This closes off a defence pattern institutions might otherwise attempt: pointing to a vendor's own testing documentation as proof of soundness. The regulatory logic mirrors SEBI's Regulation 16C sole-liability principle for securities (Section 6.2) and RBI's LSP-accountability rule for digital lending (Section 6.1) — accountability for a model's behaviour cannot be discharged by relying on someone else's assurance of it, however credible that assurance appears. This has direct, practical teeth for the sector playbooks in Chapter 6: an insurer using a specialised insurtech vendor's graph-based fraud-detection platform (Section 6.3), or an NBFC using an LSP's agentic underwriting co-pilot (Section 6.1), must independently validate that system — not simply file the vendor's certificate.

8.2.3 The Five Validation Trigger Points

RBI's draft specifies that validation is not a one-time, pre-launch event but a recurring obligation triggered at five distinct points across a model's life:

  • Prior to deployment — before the model is first used to make or influence real decisions.
  • After deployment — an initial post-launch review confirming the model behaves in production as it did in pre-launch testing (a distinction that matters because production data pipelines, latency constraints, and real customer behaviour frequently differ from test-environment conditions).
  • Following any modification — any material change to the model's logic, feature set, or training data triggers a fresh validation cycle, not merely a lighter-touch change review.
  • On internal or external triggers — e.g., a spike in customer complaints, a regulatory inquiry, an observed anomaly in monitoring dashboards, or an external event (a market shock, a new fraud typology) that calls the model's continued soundness into question.
  • Periodically, per the MRMF's own schedule — a routine, calendar-driven revalidation cadence set by the institution's own Board-approved framework, calibrated to the model's risk tier (Section 8.1.4): higher-tier models should be revalidated more frequently than lower-tier ones.

Technical implication: this means an institution's model-inventory system (Section 8.1.3) must track not just a model's current validation status but a full validation history with dates and trigger reasons — enabling the RMCB to see, for any model, when it was last validated, why, and what was found, rather than relying on a single "validated: yes/no" flag that tells a reviewer nothing about how current or how triggered that validation actually was.

8.2.4 The Four Dimensions of Validation

Each validation exercise must assess four distinct dimensions, and conflating them is one of the most common ways institutions produce validation reports that look thorough but miss material risk:

1. Inputs — data quality, assumptions, and limitations. This dimension asks whether the data feeding the model is fit for purpose: does it satisfy the classification, lineage and quality standards established in Chapter 7? Are the assumptions embedded in feature engineering (e.g., that a three-month deposit trend is a stable predictor of repayment capacity, as in Section 6.1's alternative-data scoring) explicitly documented and periodically re-tested, or silently inherited from the original model-build without ever being revisited? A validator reviewing an agentic credit-scoring pipeline should trace specific input features back through the lineage architecture (Section 7.1.3) to confirm the model is genuinely using the data it claims to use — not simply accept the developer's description of the feature set at face value.

2. Conceptual and design soundness. This is a qualitative-but-rigorous review of whether the model's underlying methodology is theoretically justified and appropriate for its intended use — for a gradient-boosted-tree credit model, whether that algorithm family is well-suited to the data volume and interpretability requirements at hand; for an LLM-based agentic underwriting co-pilot (Section 6.1's Helios), whether the foundation model's known limitations, training-data provenance, and documented failure modes have actually been examined by the validator, rather than treated as an unexaminable black box even internally. Where a model cannot be adequately explained even to the validator, that itself is a validation finding, not a reason to skip the design-soundness review.

3. Performance. This dimension is grounded in backtesting and outcomes analysis — comparing model outputs against actual, realised outcomes over a historical window. For a fraud-scoring model (Section 6.3's multi-agent architecture, Section 6.4's PhonePe Guardrails), this means checking whether flagged transactions were subsequently confirmed as fraudulent at the expected rate, and — critically — checking the false-positive and false-negative rate separately for different population segments, not merely in aggregate; a model that appears well-calibrated overall can still be systematically miscalibrated for a specific geography, product segment, or demographic group, which is precisely the AIR/SMD fairness-testing discipline (Chapter 4, Section 6.1) re-expressed as a formal performance-validation requirement. For a credit model, this extends to checking that predicted default probabilities match observed default rates across score bands — a calibration check, not merely a ranking check (i.e., does the model correctly separate good from bad borrowers and does it assign numerically accurate probabilities, which are two different and both necessary properties).

4. Alignment with intended use. A model validated as statistically sound for one purpose can still fail this dimension if it is being used for something subtly different from what it was built and tested for — for example, a credit-risk model built and validated for unsecured personal loans being repurposed, without fresh validation, to score secured SME working-capital applications (Section 6.1), where the relevant risk drivers and data distributions differ materially. This dimension exists specifically to catch model creep — the gradual, often well-intentioned expansion of a model's use beyond its original validated scope.

8.2.5 Sensitivity Analysis and Stress Testing — With an AI-Specific Extension

Beyond backtesting against historical data, validators must perform sensitivity analysis, deliberately perturbing key inputs and assumptions to observe how model outputs change, and identifying which inputs the model is most sensitive to (a model whose output swings dramatically from a small change in one input warrants closer scrutiny of that input's data quality). RBI's draft extends this with an AI-specific requirement: REs must test AI models' behavioural characteristics under stressed scenarios — deliberately feeding the model data that departs materially from its training distribution, such as a simulated economic downturn, an unusual and previously unseen transaction pattern, or an adversarially crafted input of the kind discussed in Section 7.3's poisoning-attack analysis — and confirming the model degrades predictably and safely, rather than producing erratic, overconfident, or silently wrong outputs. This connects validation directly back to the adversarial-training countermeasure described in Section 7.3.5: a validator should be able to demonstrate, with evidence, that a fraud model tested against synthetically generated evasive transaction patterns maintains acceptable detection performance, not merely assert that it "should" be robust.

8.2.6 Technical Pattern: Champion-Challenger Frameworks

A widely used technical mechanism for satisfying RBI's "validation following any modification" and "periodic validation" requirements without disrupting live production is the champion-challenger framework. Under this pattern, a "challenger" model — either a proposed replacement or an alternative methodology — is trained and run in parallel with the live "champion" model on the exact same incoming production data, with its predictions logged for comparison but never acted upon in real customer or transaction decisions.

This allows the institution to build a genuine, evidence-based case for whether a proposed model change actually improves on the current model's real-world performance, before that change is ever deployed to affect a single real decision — directly supporting RBI's requirement that model outputs "replicate and are stable in production" before go-live. For an agentic system like the PhonePe Guardrails fraud engine (Section 6.4) or L&T Finance's Cyclops underwriting engine (Section 6.1), a champion-challenger setup means a proposed model update runs silently alongside the production system for a defined observation window (commonly four to eight weeks, calibrated to transaction volume and the statistical power needed to detect a meaningful performance difference), with the challenger's outputs feeding directly into the validator's backtesting analysis (Section 8.2.4) rather than requiring a separate, disconnected test exercise.

8.2.7 Reporting Obligations and Timelines

Validation is not complete until its findings reach the right governance body within a defined window. RBI's draft imposes a hard three-month deadline: validation reports, including key findings and recommendations, must reach the RMCB (or a delegated authority, calibrated to the model's risk tier) within three months of the validation's completion. For high-risk models specifically, the RMCB itself — not a delegated committee — must review the validation report directly, reflecting the Board-level accountability structure established in Section 8.1.4's tiering discussion.

Practical implementation note: institutions should build this reporting deadline directly into the model-inventory system's workflow (Section 8.1.3) as an automated tracking field — a validation record that has not reached its designated governance body within the three-month window should trigger an automatic escalation flag, since a validation finding sitting unreviewed in a validator's file for four or five months represents exactly the kind of governance gap the RMCB oversight structure is designed to prevent.

8.2.8 Connecting Validation Back to the Sector Playbooks

The validation discipline described here gives concrete institutional teeth to obligations already discussed in Chapter 6:

  • SEBI's requirement that Black Box algo providers submit a written RMS description and auditor certificate (Section 6.2) is functionally a sector-specific validation exercise — institutions should treat that SEBI filing as one input into, not a substitute for, the fuller four-dimension RBI-style validation described here.
  • The IRDAI-anticipated pre- and post-deployment audit framework for insurance AI (Section 6.3) maps directly onto RBI's "prior to deployment" and "after deployment" validation triggers — institutions building insurance claims and fraud agents today should design their validation documentation to be reusable across both the RBI-style MRM obligation (where applicable to bank-owned insurance arms or bancassurance models) and the forthcoming IRDAI framework, rather than building two parallel, disconnected validation processes.
  • The explainability-threshold and human-oversight requirements discussed in Section 8.0's broader MRM overview are validated precisely through the conceptual-soundness and alignment-with-intended-use dimensions above — a model that cannot meet its declared explainability threshold should be flagged as a validation finding requiring enhanced monitoring and mandatory human intervention, not allowed to pass validation on performance metrics alone.

In the next section, 8.3, we turn to continuous oversight and monitoring — the ongoing discipline that keeps a validated model trustworthy after go-live, addressing RBI's third model-risk source: time-suitability, where a model that was once sound becomes unsuitable as conditions change.

8.3 Continuous Oversight and Monitoring

8.3.1 Why Monitoring Is Validation's Necessary Continuation, Not a Separate Function

Section 8.2 established that validation is not a single pre-launch event but a recurring obligation triggered at five points across a model's life. Continuous monitoring is what fills the space between those trigger points — and RBI's draft guidance is explicit that this is not a lesser, purely operational activity but a direct response to the third and most distinctive source of model risk it identifies: time-suitability, where "a model that was once sound becomes unsuitable as conditions change." Unlike model errors (caught at build time) or misuse (caught by scope-of-use controls), time-suitability risk is, by definition, invisible at the moment of validation — it can only be detected by watching the model continue to operate against a world that keeps moving. This is precisely why SR 11-7 and RBI's draft both treat ongoing monitoring as an extension of validation itself, with a shared reporting line into the RMCB, rather than as a separate, disconnected operational function owned solely by technology teams.

8.3.2 Policy Requirement: What Monitoring Must Confirm

RBI's draft frames the purpose of ongoing monitoring in two parts: first, confirming the model continues to be implemented and used as intended — i.e., that the scope-of-use and conceptual assumptions validated in Section 8.2.4 still hold in live operation; and second, evaluating whether changes in products, exposures, client behaviour, or market conditions necessitate redevelopment, adjustment, or replacement of the model. This second requirement is deliberately forward-looking: monitoring is not just a pass/fail check against the original validation baseline, but an ongoing judgment about whether the world the model was built for still resembles the world it is now operating in. A credit-scoring model built on pre-pandemic income-stability assumptions, or a fraud model trained on transaction patterns from before a major UPI feature change, are the textbook examples of models that can remain statistically "accurate" on paper while becoming genuinely unsuitable in substance — exactly the gap continuous monitoring exists to catch.

8.3.3 Core Technical Concept: Drift, and Its Three Distinct Forms

The single most important technical concept underpinning continuous monitoring is drift — a change in the statistical relationship between a model's inputs, outputs, and the real world it is meant to represent. Three distinct forms of drift require different detection techniques, and conflating them (as many institutions do) causes monitoring dashboards to miss real risk:

  • Data drift (covariate shift) — the distribution of incoming input data changes over time, even if the underlying input-output relationship itself has not. For example, if L&T Finance's Cyclops engine (Section 6.1) begins receiving a growing proportion of applications from a new customer segment (say, gig-economy borrowers with irregular income patterns) that was underrepresented in the original training data, the feature distributions shift even before any model-quality problem appears.
  • Concept drift — the underlying relationship between inputs and the true outcome changes, meaning the same input pattern that once predicted low default risk may now predict something different, because real-world borrower behaviour itself has changed (e.g., a change in bankruptcy law, a shift in gig-economy income volatility patterns, or a new UPI fraud typology emerging that exploits transaction patterns the original PhonePe Guardrails model (Section 6.4) had never seen).
  • Label/outcome drift — the definition or measurement of the outcome itself changes over time (e.g., a change in how "default" is defined internally, or a change in claims-adjudication criteria in an insurance context, Section 6.3), which can make historical validation benchmarks silently non-comparable to current performance without anyone having changed the model at all.

Technical implementation: production-grade monitoring pipelines typically track Population Stability Index (PSI) or similar distributional-distance metrics, comparing the feature distribution of live inference data against the training-data baseline on a rolling window (commonly daily or weekly), with a graduated alert threshold — a PSI below roughly 0.1 is generally considered stable, 0.1–0.25 signals moderate drift warranting investigation, and above 0.25 signals significant drift typically triggering a mandatory revalidation cycle (Section 8.2.3's "on internal trigger" pathway). This threshold-based automation is what allows an institution to satisfy RBI's monitoring obligation continuously, rather than relying on periodic manual reviews that, in practice, tend to be run too infrequently to catch drift before it causes real customer or financial harm.

8.3.4 Outcomes-Based Monitoring: Override Rates as an Early-Warning Signal

Beyond statistical drift metrics, one of the most practically useful — and most commonly underused — monitoring signals in agentic BFSI systems is the human-override rate: the frequency with which a human reviewer overturns or overrides an agent's recommendation, tracked segmented by applicant or customer profile, not merely in aggregate. A rising override rate for a specific segment (a particular geography, income band, or product type) is often the earliest available signal that a model's real-world performance has begun to diverge from its validated behaviour — appearing in the override-rate trend well before it becomes visible in aggregate accuracy or default-rate statistics, simply because those aggregate metrics take longer to accumulate enough realised outcomes to detect a shift statistically.

This is directly relevant to every human-in-the-loop pattern discussed in Chapter 6: for L&T Finance's Helios underwriting co-pilot (Section 6.1), a credit officer overriding the agent's recommendation more frequently for a specific SME sector is an actionable monitoring signal, not merely an operational nuisance to be tolerated; for the insurance claims-routing logic described in Section 6.3.2, a rising rate of human reviewers overturning the agent's structural-vs-surface-damage classification for a specific vehicle category should trigger the same escalation.

8.3.5 AI-Specific Monitoring Requirements Under RBI's Draft

RBI's draft guidance extends conventional drift and outcomes monitoring with obligations specific to AI/ML models, directly building on the reliance-and-autonomy tiering established in Section 8.1.4:

  • Continuous fairness-metric tracking, not a one-time validation check — the AIR/SMD-style testing described in Section 8.2.4 as a validation dimension must be re-run on a recurring basis against live production data, since a model that was fair at validation can drift into unfairness as its input population changes (a direct consequence of data drift, Section 8.3.3).
  • Kill-switch readiness as a monitoring precondition — the mandatory override, suspension and deactivation mechanisms required for every AI model (introduced in Section 8.0) are only meaningful if monitoring is sensitive and fast enough to trigger them before material harm accumulates; a kill switch that exists but is never actually triggered because monitoring thresholds are set too loosely is a compliance gap in substance even if the switch itself passes an audit checklist.
  • Human review of model-driven decisions to identify anomalies — RBI's draft frames this as a standing control, not exception handling, meaning a defined sample of agent decisions (not merely overridden ones) should be routinely reviewed by humans even when the agent's own confidence score suggests no problem, precisely to catch the class of failure the model itself cannot detect in its own output.
  • Behavioural stress-testing extended into production monitoring — beyond the pre-deployment stress tests described in Section 8.2.5, institutions should periodically re-run stressed-scenario probes against the current production model (not just the originally validated version), since a model's behaviour under stress can itself drift as its underlying parameters are retrained or fine-tuned over time.

8.3.6 Technical Architecture: Building the Monitoring Dashboard

A production monitoring system for a high-tier agentic model (Section 8.1.4) should combine, at minimum, four dashboard layers feeding a single, RMCB-visible reporting surface: (1) distributional monitoring — PSI/drift metrics per input feature, refreshed on a rolling basis; (2) performance monitoring — realised accuracy, calibration, and false-positive/false-negative rates, segmented by population subgroup, refreshed as outcome data becomes available; (3) fairness monitoring — AIR/SMD-style disparity metrics, tracked over time rather than as a point-in-time snapshot; and (4) behavioural/override monitoring — human-override rates and anomaly-flag rates, segmented by customer or transaction profile. Each layer should carry its own graduated alert thresholds (as illustrated for PSI in Section 8.3.3), with breaches automatically logged into the model-inventory system's "key observations" field (Section 8.1.3) and routed to trigger the "on internal trigger" validation pathway (Section 8.2.3) — closing the loop between monitoring and validation rather than treating them as parallel, disconnected workstreams.

Worked example: applying this architecture to PhonePe's Guardrails fraud engine (Section 6.4), each of the four intelligence layers — Connected, Action, Profile, and Behavioural Biometrics — would carry its own drift and performance dashboard, with the meta-model's combined risk score monitored separately for calibration drift; PhonePe's own documented outcomes (a sustained 40% fraud-rate reduction alongside a low, stable 1% reinstatement/false-positive rate) are themselves an example of exactly the kind of monitoring evidence an RMCB should expect to see presented on an ongoing basis, not merely as a one-time launch result.

8.3.7 Connecting Monitoring Back to the Sector Playbooks and Governance Structures

Continuous monitoring is where the abstractions of Chapters 3–5 and the sector-specific mechanisms of Chapter 6 become an operating routine rather than a design intention:

  • The national fraud-intelligence layers discussed in Section 6.4 — MuleHunter.AI, IDPIC's Screening API, NPCI's embedded risk-score schema — should be treated as additional monitoring inputs feeding an institution's own dashboard, not merely as one-time integrations; a divergence between an institution's internal risk score and the shared national signal for the same transaction is itself a monitoring signal worth investigating.
  • The graph-based fraud-ring detection systems in insurance (Section 6.3) require a monitoring extension beyond standard drift metrics: network-centrality thresholds (which determine how "connected" a claimant needs to be before triggering a fraud flag) should themselves be monitored for drift, since a fraud ring's structure evolves specifically to evade detection, making the graph model's own decision boundary a moving target requiring more frequent revalidation than a typical tabular model.
  • RMCB reporting cadence for monitoring findings should mirror the three-month validation-reporting deadline established in Section 8.2.7 — high-tier models should have monitoring summaries reaching the RMCB at least quarterly, with any threshold breach triggering the ad hoc escalation pathway immediately rather than waiting for the next scheduled report.

In the next section, 8.4, we turn to governance, third-party accountability and AI-specific controls — the final MRM pillar, which formalises the Board- and RMCB-level structures that receive, act on, and are ultimately accountable for everything the inventory, validation and monitoring disciplines in Sections 8.1–8.3 surface.

8.4 Governance, Third-Party Accountability and AI-Specific Controls

8.4.1 Why This Is the Closing, Not a Separate, Pillar

Sections 8.1–8.3 established what must exist (the inventory), how it must be tested (validation), and how it must be watched over time (monitoring). This final pillar answers the question that ties the other three together: who is accountable when any of it fails, and what specific controls does RBI require regardless of who built or supplied the model? RBI's draft guidance is structured so that every finding surfaced by inventory, validation, or monitoring has a defined governance destination — the Board or the Risk Management Committee of the Board (RMCB) — and a defined accountability owner, even when the model itself was never built in-house. Without this closing pillar, the earlier three become a well-documented paper trail with no one actually empowered, or obligated, to act on it.

8.4.2 The Board-RMCB Governance Architecture

RBI's draft requires every Regulated Entity to establish a Board-approved Model Risk Management Framework (MRMF) covering all models — internally built, third-party sourced, or hybrid — with the Board itself responsible for approving the institution's model-risk appetite, tolerance limits, and the tiering methodology described in Section 8.1.4. The RMCB then carries a distinct, non-delegable set of operational responsibilities sitting one level below Board policy-setting: reviewing validation reports for high-risk models (Section 8.2.7), conducting an annual review of model tiering outcomes (as opposed to the Board's review of tiering methodology), and overseeing monitoring of critical models — explicitly including any model approved via an exception pathway, any third-party model, and any AI model.

Why this two-tier split matters technically: separating "who sets the rules" (Board) from "who applies the rules to specific models on an ongoing basis" (RMCB) prevents a common governance failure mode where a Board approves a sound-looking framework document once a year, while no one at an operational cadence actually checks whether individual high-risk models are being tiered, validated, and monitored consistently with that framework. The RMCB's standing, recurring mandate is what keeps Sections 8.1–8.3 from becoming a compliance document that exists in principle but is never actually executed against the live model inventory.

8.4.3 Third-Party Accountability: Closing the "The Vendor's Model Made the Error" Defence

RBI's draft states a principle with direct, sharp consequences for BFSI institutions relying on external AI vendors, LSPs, and insurtech platforms across every sector playbook in Chapter 6: a regulated entity remains fully accountable for the outcomes of any model it uses, regardless of whether it was built internally or procured from a third party. This is not a novel principle in isolation — it is the same sole-liability logic SEBI's Regulation 16C already applies to securities intermediaries (Section 6.2) and the same LSP-accountability rule RBI's own Digital Lending Directions apply to digital lending platforms (Section 6.1) — but the MRM draft generalises it across the entire model landscape of an institution, not just lending or trading-specific models.

What this requires in practice, concretely:

  • Independent validation cannot be outsourced to the vendor's own assurance. As established in Section 8.2.2, vendor certification alone does not satisfy the validation obligation — the RE must independently validate the vendor's model using the same four-dimension framework (inputs, conceptual soundness, performance, alignment with intended use) applied to internally built models.
  • Contractual audit rights become a compliance precondition, not a negotiating nicety. An institution cannot independently validate a vendor's model, monitor its drift, or investigate a fairness finding if its vendor contract does not grant access to the data, documentation, and change-notification information required to do so. This directly extends the vendor-governance discipline introduced in Chapter 5: every AI/ML vendor contract should be reviewed specifically against the question, "does this contract give us what we need to satisfy Sections 8.1–8.3 for this model?" — and contracts that fail this test should be renegotiated or flagged as a governance gap before the vendor's model proceeds further up the risk-tier ladder.
  • The vendor relationship itself must appear in the model inventory. Section 8.1.3's mandatory metadata fields (developer, dependencies) must accurately reflect third-party sourcing — an inventory entry that lists "internal" as the developer for a model that is, in substance, a lightly customised third-party platform is itself a governance failure, obscuring exactly the accountability question RBI's principle is designed to surface.

Worked example across sector playbooks: an NBFC using an LSP's agentic underwriting platform (Section 6.1) must independently validate that platform's scoring logic and maintain "effective oversight and monitoring" over it — the RE's own name appears on the sanction letter, and RBI's LSP-accountability rule already makes this explicit for lending specifically. The MRM draft simply confirms that this is not a lending-specific quirk but the general rule: an insurer using a specialised insurtech vendor's graph-based fraud-detection platform (Section 6.3), or a broker using a third-party algo provider's Black Box trading strategy (Section 6.2), face the identical accountability structure, regardless of the sector-specific regulator ultimately enforcing it.

8.4.4 AI-Specific Controls: The Requirements Beyond Conventional MRM

Layered on top of the governance and third-party principles above, RBI's draft introduces several controls that apply specifically to AI/ML models and have no direct precedent in traditional statistical-model MRM practice — these are the requirements that most concretely operationalise the FREEAI Sutras (Chapter 3) into binding obligations:

  • Mandatory kill switches. Every AI model must have override, suspension, and deactivation mechanisms enabling instant shutdown if it produces harmful or erroneous outputs — a regulatory requirement, not merely a recommended engineering pattern. As noted in Section 8.3.5, a kill switch that exists on paper but is never actually triggered because monitoring thresholds are set too loosely is a governance failure in substance, even if it would pass a narrow audit checklist.
  • Model-specific explainability thresholds. REs must define explicit explainability/transparency thresholds calibrated to how heavily a given model relies on AI in making material decisions (directly connected to the reliance-tiering factor in Section 8.1.4); where a model cannot meet its own declared threshold, the RE must impose enhanced monitoring, additional validation and back-testing, mandatory human intervention on critical decisions, and a comparative impact assessment against existing traditional (non-AI) models as an alternative.
  • Mandatory human oversight as a standing control. AI models must never be the sole decision-maker for material outcomes; robust arrangements for human intervention, override, and periodic human review of model outputs must exist as an ongoing operational routine, not as exception handling invoked only when something has already gone wrong.
  • Customer disclosure for customer-facing and generative AI. Any customer-facing AI/ML system must disclose to the customer that they are interacting with an AI system, and must offer the option to switch to a human at any point, with additional cybersecurity controls specifically mandated for generative AI systems interfacing with customers or external users — directly reinforcing the security controls detailed in Section 7.3.
  • Behavioural stress-testing, both at validation (Section 8.2.5) and, as established in Section 8.3.5, on a recurring basis in production, since a model's behaviour under stress can itself drift as underlying parameters are retrained.

8.4.5 Bringing the Four MRM Pillars Together: An Institutional Operating Loop

Sections 8.1 through 8.4 are best understood not as four independent checklists but as a single closed operating loop: the inventory (8.1) determines what exists and how much it matters; validation (8.2) determines whether it is currently trustworthy; monitoring (8.3) determines whether it remains trustworthy over time; and governance and third-party accountability (8.4) determines who is answerable when any stage of this loop surfaces a problem, and ensures the loop cannot be broken simply because a model was sourced from outside the institution. A model that enters this loop as a low-tier, internally built spreadsheet formula and later evolves — through feature expansion, vendor integration, or a shift toward greater autonomy — into a high-tier, third-party-supplied agentic system should be re-tiered, revalidated, and re-escalated to RMCB oversight as that evolution occurs, not left running under its original, now-outdated classification.

For a BFSI RAI programme, this closing pillar is where board-level buy-in becomes operationally real: without an RMCB that actually reviews validation reports on schedule, actually questions rising override rates, and actually holds third-party vendors to the same accountability standard as internal teams, the substantial technical infrastructure described across Chapters 4, 6, 7 and 8.1–8.3 remains well-engineered but ultimately unsupervised. This closes Chapter 8's treatment of Model Risk Management, Validation and Assurance. 8.4 Assurance and Audit

8.4.1 Why Audit Is a Structurally Distinct Function From Validation

Section 8.2 established validation as an independent "second line of defence" function. RBI's draft guidance goes further, formalising a complete three-lines-of-defence structure specific to model risk: model owners as the first line (accountable for day-to-day model performance and appropriate use), an independent Model Risk Management and Validation function as the second line (the validator role examined in Section 8.2), and a robust and independent Internal Audit function as the third line of defence. This third line is the subject of this section, and it is deliberately structured to be independent of both the first and second lines — audit does not re-perform validation, it assesses whether the validation and monitoring functions themselves are operating effectively, closing the loop on whether the entire MRM apparatus described in Sections 8.1–8.3 can actually be relied upon.

A useful way to distinguish the second and third lines technically: the validator asks "is this specific model sound?"; internal audit asks "is our process for determining model soundness itself sound, consistently applied, and adequately resourced across the enterprise?" A validation report can be excellent for one model and the audit function can still find that, across the portfolio, validations are chronically late, under-resourced, or captured by undue influence from the business lines that own the models being reviewed.

8.4.2 What Internal Audit Must Specifically Assess

Drawing on RBI's draft alongside established international practice, an effective internal audit programme for AI/ML models should test across the full lifecycle, not merely the final validation sign-off:

  • Data access, collection and compliance — auditing whether the data governance controls established in Chapter 7 (classification, consent tracking, lineage) were actually followed for the models under review, not merely documented as policy.
  • Model development and validation — testing whether the validator function (Section 8.2) genuinely operated with the independence RBI's draft requires, and whether validation reports met the four-dimension standard (inputs, conceptual soundness, performance, alignment with intended use) rather than being a superficial sign-off.
  • Deployment and integration — auditing whether the model, as actually deployed in production systems, matches what was validated — a surprisingly common gap, since production integration can introduce latency shortcuts, feature substitutions, or configuration drift between what was tested and what is running live.
  • Monitoring effectiveness — testing whether the drift, fairness, and override-rate monitoring described in Section 8.3 is actually detecting the issues it claims to detect, for example by injecting a known synthetic anomaly and confirming the monitoring dashboard flags it within the expected timeframe.
  • Kill-switch functionality — periodically testing, not merely documenting, that the mandatory override/suspension/deactivation mechanisms required for every AI model (Section 8.0) actually work as designed under realistic operating conditions — a kill switch that has never been exercised in a controlled drill is an unverified control.
  • Third-party and vendor model governance — auditing whether contractual audit rights (Section 8.4 of the accountability discussion, immediately following) are being exercised in practice, not merely negotiated on paper.

8.4.3 The FREE-AI Committee's Complementary Audit Architecture

RBI's own FREE-AI Committee report (Chapter 3) proposed a detailed AI audit architecture that pre-dates and complements the MRM draft's three-lines-of-defence structure, and BFSI institutions should design their audit programme to satisfy both simultaneously. The FREE-AI report recommends REs implement a comprehensive AI audit framework, aligned with a board-approved AI risk categorisation, covering the full lifecycle — data inputs, model and algorithm, and decision outputs — structured around three components: (1) internal audit for AI applications (the third-line function discussed above), (2) third-party audits specifically for high-risk AI use cases (an additional, external assurance layer beyond internal audit for the institution's most consequential models), and (3) a periodic, biennial review of the AI audit framework itself — a meta-control ensuring the audit methodology does not become stale as AI techniques and risks evolve.

The FREE-AI report also specifies what an effective AI audit substantively examines: correct and unbiased training data, decision explainability, fairness, consistency and compliance with regulations, the presence of a functioning halt mechanism for AI-driven processes, business continuity planning for core AI systems, inspection using AI-specific checklists and risk templates, and periodic assessment of the underlying policies and procedures. Notably, the report recommends REs include AI-related disclosures in annual reports and websites, with regulators expected to specify an AI-specific disclosure standard — extending the customer-facing disclosure obligation (Section 8.0) into a formal external-reporting obligation as well.

8.4.4 Third-Party and Vendor Assurance: SOC 2 as a Necessary But Insufficient Layer

A critical, easily-misunderstood technical point for institutions relying on third-party AI vendors (LSPs, insurtech platforms, algo providers, foundation-model API providers across Chapter 6's sector playbooks): SOC 2 Type II reports and similar operational-assurance certifications address a genuinely different question than model validation, and one cannot substitute for the other. SOC 2 evaluates a vendor's operational and security controls — encryption in transit and at rest, access control, logging, and (where in scope) confidentiality and availability commitments — verified by an independent auditor over an observation period, typically 12 months for a full-cycle Type II report. It does not validate model accuracy, output quality, fairness, or bias — that assessment remains squarely the responsibility of the RE's own independent validation function (Section 8.2), regardless of how strong the vendor's SOC 2 report is.

Practical due-diligence checklist for vendor AI assurance, combining SOC 2 review with the model-risk-specific questions SOC 2 does not cover:

  • Confirm which Trust Services Criteria the vendor's SOC 2 report covers — Security is always in scope; Availability and Confidentiality are typical minimums for any lending, claims, or trading-adjacent AI vendor, given the sensitivity of the data path.
  • Review the observation period, noted exceptions, and management's responses to those exceptions — a report with unresolved exceptions in areas touching the AI data path (access control, encryption) is a red flag regardless of the vendor's model-performance claims.
  • Separately and explicitly request the vendor's model-specific documentation — training-data provenance, known failure modes, fairness-testing results, explainability methodology — since none of this appears in a SOC 2 report at all.
  • Confirm a "zero-training" contractual posture where relevant (i.e., the vendor commits not to use the institution's data to train models serving other customers), and map the full sub-processor chain, since a vendor's own AI model may itself depend on a further foundation-model provider whose practices the institution has not directly assessed.
  • Require an override audit-trail surface demoed on a real file before contracting — confirming the vendor's system actually produces the decision-trace and reasoning-capture artefacts the institution's own explainability and grievance-redressal obligations (Sections 6.1–6.4) require, rather than assuming this capability exists.

8.4.5 Reporting the Audit Findings

Audit findings on model risk should follow the same governance discipline established for validation reporting (Section 8.2.7): findings must reach the RMCB, with high-risk model audit findings requiring direct RMCB review rather than delegation, and — consistent with RBI's broader framework — any audit finding indicating a model's risk has exceeded the institution's Board-approved risk appetite should trigger the same escalation pathway as a validation or monitoring breach: enhanced controls, restrictions on use, remediation, or decommissioning, each with defined ownership and timelines.

8.5 Governance, Third-Party Accountability and AI-Specific Controls

8.5.1 Why This Is the Closing, Not a Separate, Pillar

Sections 8.1–8.4 established what must exist (the inventory), how it must be tested (validation), how it must be watched over time (monitoring), and how the entire apparatus is independently assessed (audit). This final pillar answers the question that ties everything together: who is accountable when any of it fails, and what specific controls does RBI require regardless of who built or supplied the model? RBI's draft guidance is structured so that every finding surfaced by inventory, validation, monitoring, or audit has a defined governance destination — the Board or the RMCB — and a defined accountability owner, even when the model itself was never built in-house.

8.5.2 The Board–RMCB Governance Architecture

RBI's draft requires every RE to establish a Board-approved Model Risk Management Framework (MRMF) covering all models — internally built, third-party sourced, or hybrid — with the MRMF required to cover, at minimum: taxonomy for models, governance structure, scope of model usage, model-risk tiering methodology, inventory and documentation standards, and lifecycle policies covering selection and development, validation, approval structure (including exceptions), deployment and monitoring, change management, business continuity management, and decommissioning. The Board is responsible for periodic review of the MRMF itself, approving the institution's risk appetite and tolerance for model risk, approving policies including the tiering methodology, and formally appointing the RMCB.

The RMCB then carries the operational implementation mandate: ensuring implementation and ongoing compliance with the MRMF, reviewing validation and audit reports for high-risk models, conducting the annual review of model tiering outcomes, and overseeing monitoring of critical models — explicitly including any model approved via an exception pathway, any third-party model, and any AI model. Senior management, distinct from both the Board and RMCB, is separately tasked with establishing processes and procedures, allocating human and technical resources, implementing the risk-based tiering day-to-day, maintaining the model inventory, and conducting periodic reviews.

RBI's draft also embeds an important anti-dilution principle in how tiering factors combine: a low complexity score must not disproportionately reduce a model's overall risk tier simply because it offsets a high materiality score — integration of tiering factors must not allow one factor to mask another. This directly reinforces Section 8.1.4's point about reliance and autonomy: a highly autonomous but "simple-looking" agent cannot be under-tiered merely because its individual algorithm is not statistically sophisticated.

8.5.3 Third-Party Accountability: Closing the "The Vendor's Model Made the Error" Defence

RBI's draft states a principle with direct consequences across every sector playbook in Chapter 6: a regulated entity remains fully accountable for the outcomes of any model it uses, regardless of whether it was built internally or procured from a third party, and the same comprehensive documentation standards apply to third-party models as to internally built ones. Critically, the draft anticipates the specific problem of vendor opacity: "in cases where the third-party provider does not disclose adequate information regarding the AI/ML model, the RE should identify risks that arise from such constraints, and put in place the necessary mitigants, such as limiting the usage." This is a materially stronger standard than "best-efforts due diligence" — it obligates the institution to actively restrict how it uses a vendor model when the vendor won't provide adequate transparency, rather than accepting the opacity as an unavoidable cost of using third-party AI.

This generalises the sole-liability logic already seen in SEBI's Regulation 16C (Section 6.2) and RBI's own LSP-accountability rule for digital lending (Section 6.1) across the entire model landscape of an institution. Practically, this requires: independent validation performed by the RE itself regardless of vendor certification (Section 8.2.2); contractual audit rights sufficient to satisfy Sections 8.1–8.4 (Section 8.4.4's due-diligence checklist); and accurate reflection of third-party sourcing in the model inventory's developer and dependency fields (Section 8.1.3) — an inventory entry that obscures a model's true vendor origin is itself a governance failure.

8.5.4 AI-Specific Controls: Seven Risk Dimensions Plus Human Oversight

RBI's draft specifies that REs must define the scope of AI/ML models — including foundational and frontier AI models specifically — and put in place additional controls commensurate with potential impact on customers, business operations, and financial outcomes; REs must assess whether risks arising from such models can be adequately identified, measured, monitored and managed, and deploy AI models only in business processes where that commensurate risk management is actually achievable. The draft organises AI-specific risk into a defined set of dimensions requiring individual safeguards, commonly summarised as seven AI-specific risk dimensions plus human oversight: explainability, hallucinations, bias, overfitting, spurious correlations, output variability, and data risks — each requiring a defined mitigant, layered on top of mandatory human oversight as a standing structural control.

Several controls carry specific, quotable regulatory language worth institutions building compliance documentation against directly:

  • Behavioural and adversarial testing, precisely worded. REs must "identify and address risks arising from the behavioural characteristics of AI models and implement appropriate safeguards," and specifically must "test the model behaviour under atypical or stressed scenarios to ensure vulnerabilities do not arise under edge cases, abnormal inputs, manipulations, and adversarial conditions." This directly operationalises the adversarial-robustness discussion in Section 7.3.5 and the stress-testing dimension of validation (Section 8.2.5) as an explicit regulatory mandate, not merely good practice.
  • Explainability thresholds, defined per model. REs must "define the explainability and transparency thresholds for all AI models and ensure that their outputs are explainable to the extent required" — calibrated, per Section 8.1.4's reliance factor, to how heavily a decision depends on the model's output.
  • Mandatory kill switches. Every AI model must carry override, suspension and deactivation mechanisms enabling instant shutdown — verified through the periodic drill-testing discussed in Section 8.4.2, not merely documented as an available capability.
  • Customer disclosure and human-option obligations. Customer-facing AI must disclose itself and offer a human option on request, with the grievance redressal mechanism explicitly required to address complaints arising from consumer-facing models — directly connecting this control to the grievance infrastructure discussed across Chapter 6's sector playbooks (the IRDAI Integrated Grievance Management System, Section 6.3; RBI's Digital Lending grievance norms, Section 6.1).

8.5.5 Ongoing Performance Testing: Backward- and Forward-Looking

RBI's draft specifies that ongoing model performance testing (the monitoring discipline of Section 8.3) must combine both backward-looking and forward-looking approaches, including AI-specific evaluations where applicable, and benchmarking as appropriate. Backward-looking testing is the backtesting and outcomes analysis already described in Sections 8.2.4 and 8.3; forward-looking testing extends this to scenario analysis and stress-testing projections of how the model is expected to behave under conditions not yet observed in historical data — a distinction that matters specifically for the fast-evolving fraud and adversarial-manipulation risks discussed in Section 7.3, where waiting for a new attack pattern to appear in historical data before testing against it would leave the institution perpetually one step behind.

8.5.6 Bringing the Five MRM Sections Together: An Institutional Operating Loop

Sections 8.1 through 8.5 form a single closed operating loop, now completed with the three-lines-of-defence structure formalised in this final pillar: the inventory (8.1) determines what exists and how much it matters; validation (8.2, first and second lines) determines whether it is currently trustworthy; monitoring (8.3) determines whether it remains trustworthy over time; audit (8.4, third line) independently verifies that the first two lines are actually functioning as designed; and governance and third-party accountability (8.5) determines who is answerable when any stage of this loop surfaces a problem, ensuring the loop cannot be broken simply because a model was sourced from outside the institution. A model that enters this loop as a low-tier, internally built spreadsheet formula and later evolves — through feature expansion, vendor integration, or a shift toward greater autonomy — into a high-tier, third-party-supplied agentic system should be re-tiered, revalidated, re-audited and re-escalated to RMCB oversight as that evolution occurs.

11. From Principles to Practice

11.1 Setting the Context: A Unified Compass for RAI in BFSI

Indian BFSI institutions now navigate Responsible AI using four converging compasses:

  • RBI’s FREEAI Framework, which distils responsible AI into 7 Sutras — Trust is the foundation, People First, Innovation over Restraint, Fairness and Equity, Accountability, Understandable by Design, and Safety, Resilience and Sustainability — and 26 recommendations organised across six pillars: Infrastructure, Policy, Capacity, Governance, Protection and Assurance.
  • NITI Aayog’s Responsible AI work, which frames AI around principles of safety and reliability, equality, inclusivity and nondiscrimination, privacy and security, transparency, accountability, and reinforcement of positive human values, coupled with an “Operationalising Principles” document that describes how those values should be embedded in lifecycle practices and governance.
  • The DPDP Act and Rules, which impose eight–ten core obligations on every Data Fiduciary — consent and notice, purpose limitation, data minimisation, accuracy, storage limitation, security safeguards, breach notification, accountability — plus enhanced obligations for Significant Data Fiduciaries (DPO, DPIA, independent audit).
  • RBI’s 2026 Model Risk Management draft, which translates model governance into a Boardapproved MRM framework, risktiering, independent validation, continuous oversight, and AIspecific controls such as kill switches, explainability thresholds and behavioural stresstesting.

This chapter describes how a BFSI institution can translate those principles and regulatory anchors into a coherent operating model and timebound implementation plan, drawing together the sector playbooks (Chapter 6), the data/privacy/security substrate (Chapter 7), and the MRM disciplines (Chapter 8) into a single “from principles to practice” loop.

11.2 Board-Level RAI Strategy

FREEAI and RBI’s MRM draft both explicitly locate AI governance at the Board level. A BFSI institution’s RAI strategy begins with a Boardapproved AI policy and MRM framework that:

  • Sets out where the institution intends to use AI and agentic systems — and where it will not — across credit, markets, insurance, payments and internal operations.
  • Articulates a modelrisk appetite and tolerance, including how risk tiers are defined and what levels of autonomy and reliance are acceptable for different decision types.
  • Maps FREEAI Sutras and NITI principles to concrete internal policies and controls:
  • Trust / People First: mandatory humanintheloop and override rights for highimpact decisions (loan denials, claim repudiations, trading strategies).
  • Fairness & Equity / Inclusivity: AIR/SMD-style fairness testing for credit, pricing and claims models before deployment and periodically thereafter.
  • Accountability: explicit statement that accountability for AI decisions rests with the deploying entity, never the model or vendor — mirroring FREEAI, DPDP’s Data Fiduciary concept, and SEBI’s Regulation 16C.
  • Understandable by Design / Transparency: explainability thresholds per model, adverseaction reason codes for customers, and documentation available for audit and external scrutiny.
  • Safety, Resilience and Sustainability: stresstesting, kill switches, incident reporting, businesscontinuity planning for core AI systems.

Board and RMCB agendas should include recurring items on AI adoption, RA impact assessments, fairness/explainability metrics, incident reports, and audit findings, ensuring AI is governed with the same seriousness as capital, liquidity and credit risk.

11.3 The RAI Operating Model

At the operational level, a BFSI institution’s RAI operating model aligns three regulatory spines: FREEAI’s Governance/Protection/Assurance pillars, RBI’s threelines MRM structure, and DPDP’s Data Fiduciary and SDF expectations.

Key components include:

  • AI / RAI Steering Committee under the RMCB — with representation from business lines, risk, compliance, IT, data, legal and consumer protection — responsible for AI usecase intake, RA impact assessment, prioritisation, and FREEAI alignment.
  • Model Risk & Validation function (second line) — independent of development and use — covering all models, traditional and AI/ML/agentic, with responsibility for validation, tiering, monitoring standards and killswitch design.
  • Data Protection & Privacy function — typically led by a DPO for Significant Data Fiduciaries — owning DPIAs, DPDP notice/consent design, minimisation/retention/localisation controls, vendor DPDP clauses, and breach response.
  • Internal Audit function (third line) — with an AIspecific audit plan that tests whether data governance, validation, monitoring and kill switches are effective and independently documented, drawing on FREEAI’s Assurance pillar and RBI’s audit expectations.
  • A RAI Programme Office — coordinating crosssector implementation (Chapter 6), data and privacy controls (Chapter 7), and MRM actions (Chapter 8), avoiding fragmented initiatives in separate silos.

This operating model ensures every agentic or AIenabled use case passes through common gates: intake and RA impact assessment, data/privacy review, model validation and tiering, monitoring design, and audit/assurance scheduling.

11.4 A 12–24 Month Implementation Roadmap

11.4.1 Phase 1 (0–6 Months): Foundations

In the first phase, institutions focus on establishing visibility and basic controls:

  • Conduct a comprehensive model discovery and inventory using RBI’s broad model definition, capturing statistical models, rules engines, spreadsheets, vendor algorithms and agentic systems across the enterprise.
  • Populate mandatory metadata (owner, developer, validator, approver, intended use, dependencies) and assign risk tiers using complexity, materiality, reliance and autonomy as separate scored factors.
  • Adopt and approve a Boardlevel AI policy and updated MRMF, explicitly referencing FREEAI Sutras, NITI RA principles, DPDP obligations, and sectoral requirements (RBI, SEBI, IRDAI).
  • Establish core data governance capabilities (classification, lineage, minimisation, retention and deletion schedules, localisation of payment data) as described in Chapter 7, with automated jobs enforcing DPDP storage limitation and logretention rules.
  • Build initial monitoring dashboards for the most material models (e.g., top credit, fraud, claims, trading models), tracking drift, calibration, fairness metrics and override rates.

11.4.2 Phase 2 (6–12 Months): Sector Playbook Pilots

The second phase uses the sectorspecific playbooks (Chapter 6) to demonstrate full RA lifecycle operation:

  • Select 1–2 pilot use cases per sector — for example:
  • Digital lending underwriting and collections (Section 6.1).
  • Retail algo trading or roboadvice in securities (Section 6.2).
  • Motor claims photogrammetry plus multiagent fraud detection in insurance (Section 6.3).
  • UPI fraud detection and agentic payment/BNPL decisions in payments/fintech (Section 6.4).
  • For each pilot, run the endtoend RA lifecycle: RA impact assessment (ethical, conduct, fairness, inclusion), DPIA where DPDP requires it, data minimisation and consent design, model validation and stresstesting, monitoring and killswitch implementation, grievance and disclosure integration.
  • Implement FREEAI recommendations at usecase level: Boardapproved AI policy applied to the pilot, AI sandboxes for controlled experimentation, AI disclosures, grievance pathways, vendorrisk management and fairness/explainability checks.
  • Embed DPDP obligations — particularly SDF requirements — in pilots where the institution qualifies as a Significant Data Fiduciary, ensuring DPIAs, DPO oversight and independent audits are aligned with RA impact assessments rather than duplicative.

11.4.3 Phase 3 (12–24 Months): Scaling and External Assurance

In the third phase, institutions industrialise and extend controls:

  • Scale the RA lifecycle gates (intake, RAIA, validation, monitoring, audit, decommissioning) to cover all Tier2 and Tier3 models, with standardised templates and workflows.
  • Integrate FREEAI’s Assurance pillar into audit and reporting: recurring AI audits, AI incident reporting formats, and AI-related sections in annual reports describing governance, use cases, consumer safeguards and complaints.
  • Formalise thirdparty assurance processes: vendor duediligence and SOC 2 reviews for AI vendors, AIBOM/SBOM for AI components, and explicit internal validation and monitoring plans for critical vendor models.

11.5 Applying Sector Playbooks: From Pilot to Standard Practice

Chapter 11 ties back to the sector chapters by showing how each playbook can progress from pilot to standard operating pattern.

Banking and NBFCs. Institutions embed the digital lending and collections playbook by:

  • Ensuring all DLAs, KFS and coolingoff features comply with RBI’s Digital Lending Directions and FAQs.
  • Integrating AA/UPI/GST alternative data with documented necessity reviews and DPDP-compliant consent per source.
  • Operationalising SHAP-based reason codes for adverse decisions and AIR/SMD fairness testing across credit segments.

Securities and Capital Markets. Institutions operationalise SEBI’s regime by:

  • Aligning internal algo inventory with SEBI’s AI/ML reporting and retailalgo framework, correctly classifying TWAP/VWAP vs Black Box algos.
  • Engineering static IP, OAuth/2FA, RMS checks and kill switches into API and infrastructure layers, with monitoring for OPS thresholds and abnormal behaviour.
  • Applying suitability and disclosure obligations to roboadvice and AIassisted research flows.

Insurance. Insurers embed the insurance playbook by:

  • Registering and classifying claims and fraud agents (photogrammetry, multiagent fraud stacks, graph models) in the model inventory.
  • Enforcing reasoning capture, humanintheloop gates for highimpact claim decisions, and fairness monitoring for pricing and fraud flags.
  • Integrating IRDAI policyholderprotection TATs and grievance infrastructure with agent decision logs.

Payments, Fintech and NewAge Players. Payments operators and fintechs embed the payments playbook by:

  • Integrating NPCI <RiskScores> and IDPIC Screening APIs into frauddecisioning, while respecting protocollevel polling and reversal limits.
  • Designing fraud engines along multilayer patterns (e.g., PhonePe’s Guardrails: connected, action, profile, behavioural layers) with edge/ondevice inference and DPDP-compliant minimisation.
  • Hardcoding conversational compliance (Fair Practices Code calltime windows, NDNC scrubbing, consent withdrawal) into servicing and collections agents.

11.6 Metrics, Reporting and Culture

To demonstrate that principles have truly become practice, Chapter 11 closes with metrics and behaviours:

Structural metrics

  • Percentage of material models recorded in the inventory and assigned risk tiers.
  • Percentage of hightier models with current (≤12 months) validation and live monitoring dashboards.
  • Percentage of AI/ML models with tested kill switches and documented humanoverride procedures.

Outcome metrics

  • Fraudloss reductions and falsepositive rates (e.g., PhonePe’s reported 40% drop in fraud complaints and ~1% reinstatement/falsepositive rate).
  • Fairness metrics (AIR/SMD) trending within acceptable bands across credit, pricing and claims segments.
  • DPDP incidents and breachresponse time (72hour DPB notifications, registered grievances and resolutions).

Behavioural indicators

  • Board and RMCB time spent on AI/RAI, measured through agenda items and decisions.
  • Number and quality of RA impact assessments completed before launching AI use cases.
  • Staff training completion rates on RA, DPDP and AI security, plus frequency and quality of AI incident drills and killswitch tests.

Taken together, these elements show when a BFSI institution has moved from complianceoriented statements to a living RAI operating system — one in which FREEAI’s Sutras, NITI RA’s principles, DPDP’s obligations and RBI’s MRM guidance are reflected in daytoday decisions, artefacts, dashboards and behaviours across banking, markets, insurance and payments.
